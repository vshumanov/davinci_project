FIRST AID UNDER PRESSURE

WHAT IT IS

First aid under pressure is the small set of things an
ordinary person can do in the first few minutes of a medical
emergency to keep someone alive until trained help arrives. It
is not medicine and it is not diagnosis. It is a short list of
high-leverage actions aimed at the handful of problems that
kill quickly: catastrophic bleeding, a blocked airway, stopped
breathing, and a stopped heart. The whole discipline rests on
one blunt fact. For the worst emergencies, the gap between
collapse and death is measured in minutes, far shorter than
the time it takes an ambulance to arrive, so the only person
who can save a life in that window is whoever is already
there.

This booklet is an orientation, not a substitute for hands-on
training. Reading about compressions is not the same as having
pressed on a mannequin's chest under a coach's eye, and real
certification from a recognized first aid course is worth far
more than any text. Guidelines also differ slightly between
countries and are revised over time. Treat what follows as a
map of the territory and the reasoning behind it, so that a
proper course makes sense and so that, if you are ever the
only one there, you have a frame to act within instead of
freezing.

The single most important idea is that doing something
structured and imperfect almost always beats doing nothing
while you wait for certainty. Bystanders freeze mainly because
they are afraid of doing the wrong thing. In a true emergency,
the wrong thing is usually inaction.

WHY IT MATTERS

The arithmetic is stark. A person bleeding from a major artery
can lose enough blood to die in a few minutes. A brain starved
of oxygen begins to suffer damage within about four to six
minutes and the damage becomes severe soon after. Ambulance
response times in most places are longer than that even on a
good day. This means that for the fastest killers, survival is
decided before the professionals arrive, by whoever is
standing there. Trained bystanders are the real first
responders; the ambulance crew are the second.

It matters because the actions that save lives in that window
are simple enough for anyone to learn and require almost no
equipment. Pressing hard on a wound, tilting a head to open an
airway, pushing rhythmically on a chest: none of these need a
medical degree, and all of them have moved people from
near-certain death to full recovery. The reason more lives are
not saved is not that the techniques are hard but that most
people have never learned them and are paralyzed by fear and
uncertainty when the moment comes.

It matters, finally, as a piece of self-reliance. A person who
knows how to control bleeding and support breathing is not
helpless when something goes wrong far from a hospital, on a
trail, at a crash site, in a disaster. That competence extends
to protecting others and to staying functional, instead of
panicking, in exactly the situations where panic is most
dangerous. Knowing what to do is also what lets you stay calm,
and staying calm is itself a medical intervention.

THE ORDER OF OPERATIONS

Emergencies feel like chaos, so the professionals impose an
order, a fixed sequence of priorities that you work through in
the same way every time. The point of a fixed order is that
under stress you do not have to think about what to do first;
you fall back on the drill. A widely taught sequence for
serious trauma is remembered as MARCH: Massive hemorrhage,
Airway, Respiration, Circulation, and Head injury or
Hypothermia. The deeper logic is simply to deal with the
fastest killer first and work down.

Before any of that, the first action is always to protect
yourself and assess the scene. You are no use to anyone if you
become a second casualty. Check for traffic, fire,
electricity, violence, water, or anything else that could harm
you, and make the scene safe or move to safety before you
touch the patient. This is not cowardice; it is the reason
there is one patient instead of two. Then, if others are
present, point at a specific person and tell them clearly to
call emergency services and, if available, to fetch a
defibrillator, because a vague shout to the crowd often means
no one acts.

With the scene safe and help summoned, you check the person.
Are they responsive? Speak to them, tap their shoulders. If
they respond and are talking, their airway is open and they
are breathing, which already rules out the two most immediate
killers and lets you turn to bleeding and other injuries. If
they do not respond, you move quickly through the airway,
breathing, and circulation checks that follow. The order
matters because it front-loads the things that kill in minutes
and defers the things that can wait.

STOPPING THE BLEED

Life-threatening bleeding is the one emergency where you often
must act before anything else, because it can kill faster than
a blocked airway. The signs are blood that is spurting,
pooling, or soaking through clothing fast, or a wound to a
limb from which blood is flowing freely. The response is
direct, firm, and immediate.

1) Press hard, directly on the wound, with whatever is at
hand: a clean cloth, a shirt, or a gloved hand. Most bleeding,
even from fairly large wounds, stops with firm, sustained,
direct pressure. The commonest mistake is pressing too gently
and giving up too soon.

2) Do not keep lifting the dressing to check. Lifting disturbs
the clot that is forming. If blood soaks through, add more
material on top and keep pressing; do not remove what is
already there.

3) For a limb that is bleeding catastrophically and will not
stop with pressure, a tourniquet can be lifesaving. It goes
high on the limb, above the wound, and is tightened until the
bleeding stops, which will hurt. Note the time it was applied.
The old fear that a tourniquet means losing the limb is
outdated; modern practice accepts that a tourniquet left on
for a couple of hours is a reasonable price for not bleeding
to death, and in that case a life clearly outranks a limb.

4) For bleeding where a tourniquet cannot go, such as the
groin, armpit, or neck junctions, pack the wound by stuffing
clean cloth or gauze firmly into it and pressing hard from the
outside.

The principle under all of this is to put pressure between the
person and the loss of their blood, and to keep it there.
Blood is not something you can replace at the scene, so the
entire goal is to stop it leaving.

BREATHING AND THE STOPPED HEART

If a person is unresponsive, your next concern is whether they
are breathing. Open the airway by tilting the head back gently
and lifting the chin, which lifts the tongue off the back of
the throat, the commonest cause of a blocked airway in an
unconscious person. Then look, listen, and feel for normal
breathing for up to ten seconds. Be wary of occasional
gasping, called agonal breathing, which can appear in the
first minutes of cardiac arrest and is not real breathing;
treat it as absence of breathing.

If an unresponsive person is breathing normally, place them on
their side in the recovery position, which keeps the airway
open and lets fluid drain rather than choke them, and monitor
them until help arrives. If an unresponsive person is not
breathing normally, their heart has very likely stopped, and
you begin cardiopressions, the single most important skill in
all of first aid.

Chest compressions manually pump blood when the heart cannot.
The current adult guidance is straightforward:

1) Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, on
the breastbone, with the other hand on top.

2) Push hard and fast, straight down, compressing the chest
about five to six centimeters, roughly two inches, then let it
come all the way back up between pushes.

3) Aim for a rate of one hundred to one hundred twenty
compressions per minute. The old song Stayin' Alive is close
to that tempo, which is a genuinely useful memory aid.

4) If you are trained and willing, give two rescue breaths
after every thirty compressions. If you are not, give
continuous compressions without stopping, because hands-only
CPR still works and is far better than nothing.

5) Do not stop except to let a defibrillator analyze or shock,
or when the person recovers or help takes over. Swap with
another rescuer about every two minutes if you can, because
good compressions are exhausting and quality falls as you
tire.

An automated external defibrillator, or AED, is the other half
of surviving cardiac arrest, and it is designed to be used by
untrained people. It talks you through every step, analyzes
the heart rhythm itself, and will only deliver a shock if one
is needed. If one is available, send someone for it early and
turn it on as soon as it arrives; it will not let you shock
someone who should not be shocked. Compressions keep the brain
alive; the defibrillator is often what actually restarts a
usable heartbeat.

SHOCK AND THE MINUTES AFTER

Once the immediate killers are handled, the next danger is
shock, which in the medical sense is not emotional distress
but a failure of the circulation to deliver enough blood to
the organs, often from heavy blood loss. Its signs include
pale, cold, clammy skin, rapid shallow breathing, a fast weak
pulse, confusion, and thirst. Shock can kill after the obvious
bleeding looks controlled, so it must be watched for.

The first aid for shock is to keep the person lying down, keep
them warm with a coat or blanket since the cold worsens their
condition, reassure them, and keep monitoring their breathing
and responsiveness while you wait for the ambulance. Do not
give them anything to eat or drink, however much they ask,
because they may need surgery and because a reduced level of
consciousness makes choking a real risk. Keeping a severely
injured person calm, still, and warm is a genuine treatment,
not merely comfort.

Throughout, keep reassessing. A person who was talking can
deteriorate; bleeding you thought was controlled can restart;
breathing can stop. The emergency is not a single snapshot but
a moving situation, and the job of the first aider is to keep
watching and keep adjusting until someone with more training
and more equipment takes over. Handing over a clear, calm
summary of what happened and what you did is itself part of
the care.

CHOKING, BURNS, AND BREAKS

A blocked airway from a swallowed object is its own fast
emergency with its own drill. If a conscious, choking person
can still cough forcefully, let them cough, because coughing
is the most powerful way to clear an obstruction and nothing
you do by hand will beat it. If they cannot cough, speak, or
breathe, the airway is fully blocked and you act at once,
alternating firm blows between the shoulder blades with
abdominal thrusts, pulling inward and upward just above the
navel, to drive the object out. If they lose consciousness,
lower them carefully and begin the same chest compressions
used for cardiac arrest, which also help expel the blockage.

For burns, the single most useful action is to cool the burn
under cool running water for many minutes, which limits how
deep the damage goes, then cover it loosely with a clean,
non-stick dressing. Do not use ice, which can deepen the
injury, and do not burst blisters or smear on butter, creams,
or folk remedies, which invite infection and complicate real
treatment. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing near the
burn before swelling sets in, unless they are stuck to the
wound, in which case leave them for the professionals.

For a suspected broken bone or sprain, the aim is to stop it
moving and limit swelling, not to set it. Support the limb in
the position you found it, immobilize it if you must move the
person, apply something cold wrapped in cloth, and get medical
help. Never try to push a protruding bone back under the skin
or force a bent joint straight. As with every injury, keep
watching the whole person, because a dramatic, painful limb
can pull your attention away from a quieter but far deadlier
problem with their breathing or their bleeding.

Two more situations deserve a ready plan. For a suspected
poisoning or overdose, do not try to make the person vomit,
which often causes more harm, but find out what they took and
how much, and call emergency services or a poison line for
instructions, because the right response depends entirely on
the substance. For a stroke, speed is everything: sudden
drooping of the face, weakness in one arm, or slurred speech
means you call for help immediately and note the time the
symptoms began, because the treatments that rescue brain
tissue work only within a narrow window.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

The biggest error is hesitation. People freeze, afraid of
lawsuits, of hurting the patient, or of doing it imperfectly.
For the dying, imperfect action beats perfect inaction almost
every time, and in many places Good Samaritan protections
exist precisely to encourage bystanders to help. You will not
make a dead-level emergency worse by trying; you may well make
it better.

A second error is being too gentle. Effective compressions
crack cartilage and are tiring, and effective pressure on a
wound hurts. Beginners routinely push too softly on the chest
and press too lightly on bleeding, afraid of causing pain,
which makes both interventions useless. In a genuine
emergency, the force required is more than feels polite.

A third error is a set of persistent myths. You do not put
something in the mouth of someone having a seizure; you clear
the area and protect their head and let it pass. You do not
tilt a nosebleed head backward; you lean it forward so blood
does not run into the throat. You do not rush to remove a
tourniquet or a motorcyclist's helmet. And you do not move
someone with a possible spinal injury unless they are in
immediate danger where they lie. Many instinctive helpful
actions are actively harmful, which is exactly why the trained
drill exists.

A fourth error is forgetting your own safety and the simple
act of calling for help. Diving into traffic or into a
confined space to reach a casualty regularly produces a second
victim. And the most powerful thing a lone bystander can do is
often just to get skilled help and equipment moving early,
because everything you do at the scene is a bridge to the
treatment that truly fixes the problem.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

First aid connects to situational awareness, because
recognizing an emergency early, reading the scene for danger,
and anticipating how a patient might deteriorate are all the
same perceive-understand-predict skill applied to a body
instead of a battlefield. The first aider who sees shock
coming before it arrives buys precious minutes.

It connects to the broader family of self-reliance and
survival skills, where the ability to manage injury without
immediate access to hospitals is foundational, and to the
physiology of the body, since understanding why bleeding,
airway, and circulation are the priorities comes straight from
understanding how oxygen reaches the cells. It connects, too,
to the psychology of performing under pressure: the reason
first aid is drilled as a fixed sequence is the same reason
pilots and soldiers drill, so that when fear floods the system
and clear thinking fails, trained hands carry out the right
actions anyway. The calm that training produces is not a
personality trait. It is a skill, and like the techniques
themselves, it can be learned.
