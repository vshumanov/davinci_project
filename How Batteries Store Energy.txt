HOW BATTERIES STORE ENERGY

WHAT IT IS

A battery stores energy as chemistry and releases it as
electricity on demand. Inside, it holds substances that very
much want to react with each other, and it keeps them apart in
a way that forces the only path between them to run through
your circuit. When you close that circuit, the reaction
proceeds, but the electrons it releases are made to take the
long way round, through your phone or your flashlight, doing
useful work on the way, before completing the reaction. A
battery is, at heart, a controlled chemical reaction with the
electrons rerouted through you.

Every battery has the same three essential parts. There is the
negative electrode, the anode, made of a material that readily
gives up electrons. There is the positive electrode, the
cathode, made of a material that readily accepts them. And
between them is the electrolyte, a substance that lets charged
atoms, ions, move through it but blocks electrons. The
electrolyte is the clever part. By allowing ions through while
stopping electrons, it forces the electrons to travel through
the external wire instead, which is exactly the current you
use. Separate the two electrodes with something that passes
ions but not electrons, and the only way for the reaction to
finish is through your device.

The reaction itself is a transfer of electrons from one
material to another, which chemists call oxidation and
reduction, or redox. Oxidation is losing electrons, happening
at the anode; reduction is gaining them, happening at the
cathode. The battery's voltage, its electrical push, comes
from how badly the anode material wants to shed electrons and
the cathode wants to take them. A bigger difference in that
appetite means a higher voltage. This is why the choice of
materials, not the size of the battery, sets its voltage: a
single cell of a given chemistry always produces about the
same voltage, whether it is tiny or huge.

WHY IT MATTERS

Batteries are the technology that cut energy loose from the
wire. Before portable stored power, electricity had to be used
where and when it was generated, tethered to the grid.
Batteries let you carry energy in your pocket, which is the
hidden premise of nearly every portable device: phones,
laptops, watches, hearing aids, power tools, medical implants,
and electric cars all exist because energy can be stored
chemically and released cleanly. The entire mobile world rests
on the humble idea of keeping two eager chemicals apart until
you need them.

They matter more than ever because the shift to renewable
energy is, in large part, a battery problem. The sun and wind
produce power at nature's schedule, not ours, so storing that
energy for when it is actually needed is the central challenge
of a clean grid. Grid-scale batteries smooth the gap between
when power is generated and when it is used. The same
chemistry that runs your phone, scaled up enormously, is one
of the keys to decarbonizing electricity, which is why battery
research is among the most consequential engineering of the
century.

They matter, finally, as a window into electrochemistry, the
science at the border of chemistry and electricity that also
governs corrosion, electroplating, fuel cells, and the
signaling of your own nerves. The 2019 Nobel Prize in
Chemistry went to John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham, and
Akira Yoshino for the lithium-ion battery, a recognition that
this was not mere engineering but a deep achievement that
reshaped the world. Understanding how a battery works is
understanding a fundamental way that matter and energy trade
places.

THE CORE MECHANISM

Follow the electrons through a discharging battery. At the
anode, the material undergoes oxidation, giving up electrons
and releasing positive ions into the electrolyte. The freed
electrons cannot cross the electrolyte, so they pile up at the
anode terminal, which becomes the negative end. When you
connect a circuit, those electrons rush through the wire
toward the cathode, and that rush is the current that powers
your device. Meanwhile, inside the battery, the positive ions
drift through the electrolyte from anode to cathode, keeping
the charge balanced. At the cathode, the arriving electrons
and the arriving ions meet and the material undergoes
reduction, completing the reaction. Electrons through the
outside, ions through the inside, both flowing because the
chemistry pulls them.

The energy you get out was stored in the chemical bonds. The
products of the reaction sit at a lower energy than the
starting materials, and that difference in energy is what
pushes the electrons through your circuit. When the reactive
materials are used up, the reaction cannot proceed, no more
electrons flow, and the battery is flat. It has not lost its
stuff; it has rearranged its chemistry into the low-energy,
used-up state, and there is simply no appetite left to drive a
current.

A rechargeable battery is one whose reaction can be run
backward. By forcing current into the battery from an external
source, a charger, you push the electrons and ions back the
way they came, driving the reaction uphill and restoring the
original high-energy materials. Not all chemistries can do
this cleanly; in a disposable battery the reaction or the
physical structure cannot be reversed without damage, so once
spent it is done. The genius of a good rechargeable chemistry
is that the materials can be built up and torn down thousands
of times without falling apart, which is a demanding
requirement and the reason good rechargeable batteries are
hard to make.

Lithium-ion batteries, the ones in most modern electronics,
work by a particularly elegant version of this called
intercalation. Lithium ions are small and shuttle back and
forth between two host structures, sliding into spaces in the
cathode during discharge and back into the anode, usually
graphite, during charging, like guests checking in and out of
a layered hotel without demolishing the building. Because the
ions just move in and out of stable structures rather than
forming and breaking a messy reaction, the process can repeat
many hundreds of times. This shuttling of lithium is why they
are called lithium-ion, and it is the trick that made
lightweight, long-lived rechargeable batteries possible.

A TOUR OF REAL BATTERIES

The story begins with Alessandro Volta, who around 1800
stacked discs of two different metals separated by
brine-soaked cloth and produced the first steady electric
current, the voltaic pile. Before him, electricity was a brief
spark; Volta made it flow. His insight, that two dissimilar
metals in a conducting liquid generate a current, is the seed
of every battery since, and his name survives in the volt.

The lead-acid battery, invented in the mid-1800s, still starts
nearly every gasoline car. It uses lead and lead oxide plates
in sulfuric acid, and its great virtues are that it is cheap,
reliable, and can deliver a huge burst of current to crank an
engine. Its vices are that it is heavy and has modest energy
for its weight, which is fine for a car that carries it anyway
but useless for a phone. The alkaline cell, the familiar
disposable AA, uses zinc and manganese dioxide and is cheap,
stable, and long-lasting on the shelf, but not practically
rechargeable, which is why it ends up in the bin.

The lithium-ion cell, commercialized around 1990 after the
work that would later win the Nobel, changed everything by
packing far more energy into far less weight than anything
before, while surviving hundreds of recharge cycles. That
combination, light, dense, and rechargeable, is precisely what
a pocket computer or an electric car needs, and it is why
lithium-ion displaced the older chemistries from almost every
portable role. The trade is that lithium is reactive and the
cells are less forgiving of abuse, which is the dark side of
their power.

WHY THEY DIE, SWELL, AND BURN

Batteries age because the repeated building and tearing down
of their materials is never perfectly clean. Each charge and
discharge leaves tiny irreversible changes: unwanted side
reactions consume a little of the active material, protective
films thicken, and the electrode structures slowly crack and
lose contact. Over hundreds of cycles these small losses add
up, and the battery holds less and less charge until it is no
longer useful. This is why an old phone battery drains so
fast: not because it leaks, but because the chemistry has
partly worn out and less of it can still take part.

Swelling comes from gas. When a cell is overcharged,
overheated, or simply old, side reactions produce gas inside
the sealed pouch, and with nowhere to go it bloats the
battery. A swollen battery is a warning sign that the internal
chemistry has gone wrong and the cell should be retired
carefully, because it is now both damaged and under pressure.

Fire is the most dangerous failure, and it comes from a
vicious circle called thermal runaway. If a lithium cell gets
too hot, from a short circuit, physical damage, a
manufacturing flaw, or overcharging, a reaction begins that
releases heat, and that heat speeds up the reaction, which
releases more heat, and so on, faster and faster, until the
cell vents flammable gas and can ignite. Because lithium cells
pack so much energy so densely and contain flammable
electrolyte, once runaway starts it is hard to stop, and the
fire can reignite. This is why damaged lithium batteries are
treated as hazards, why they are regulated on aircraft, and
why good cells include separators and control electronics
specifically to prevent the runaway from ever starting. The
very density of energy that makes lithium batteries wonderful
is what makes their failures violent.

ENERGY DENSITY, CHARGE RATES, AND PACKS

A sobering number explains both why batteries are so useful
and why they are not yet the answer to everything: their
energy density. A kilogram of gasoline holds roughly forty to
fifty times as much energy as a kilogram of the best
lithium-ion battery. This gap is why a petrol car can be
refueled in minutes and driven for hundreds of miles on a tank
that weighs little, while an electric car carries a battery
pack weighing hundreds of kilograms and takes far longer to
charge. Batteries have improved steadily, but they are
climbing toward limits set by chemistry, and the stubbornness
of this comparison is the central engineering fact behind the
pace of electrification. It is also why batteries dominate
where their cleanliness, quiet, and precise control matter
most, and struggle where sheer energy per kilogram is king,
such as long-haul aviation.

How fast a battery can be charged or discharged is described
by its rate, often given relative to its capacity. Draw too
much current and the cell heats up and ages faster; charge too
fast, especially when cold, and you risk plating metallic
lithium on the electrode, which permanently degrades the cell
and can create the internal short that triggers a fire. This
is why fast charging slows down as the battery fills and why
chargers taper the current near the top, and why the last
stretch from eighty to a hundred percent takes
disproportionately long. The cell is being coaxed, not forced,
because forcing it shortens its life.

Few devices run on a single cell. Because one lithium cell
produces only a few volts, cells are wired in series to add
their voltages up to what a motor or laptop needs, and in
parallel to add their capacities for longer runtime. A laptop
or an electric car therefore contains a pack of many cells,
overseen by a battery management system, a small computer that
monitors each cell's voltage and temperature, balances them so
none is overcharged or overdrained, and cuts the circuit if
anything goes dangerously wrong. Much of what makes a modern
battery safe and long-lived is not the chemistry alone but
this electronic guardian watching every cell, because in a
large pack the weakest cell sets the fate of the whole.

The end of a battery's life raises its own set of questions.
The materials in a lithium cell, including lithium, cobalt,
and nickel, are valuable and in some cases scarce or mined
under troubling conditions, so a spent pack is not merely
waste but a concentrated ore worth recovering. Recycling can
reclaim much of the metal, and packs retired from electric
cars, still holding most of their capacity, often get a second
life storing energy for buildings and the grid before they are
finally broken down. As batteries multiply across the world,
this back end, recovering and reusing their materials rather
than discarding them, is becoming as important as the
chemistry that made them, and it closes the loop on a
technology whose front end already reshaped modern life.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

The first misconception is that a battery stores electricity,
like a tank holding charge. It does not store electrons
waiting to flow; it stores chemical potential, and it
manufactures the current on the spot by running a reaction. A
charged battery and a dead one contain the same number of
electrons; what differs is the chemical arrangement and thus
the appetite to drive them around a circuit. Thinking of a
battery as a bucket of electricity leads to confusion about
almost everything it does.

A second misconception is the lingering memory-effect advice,
that you should fully drain a battery before recharging. That
applied to certain older chemistries and is actively bad for
lithium-ion, which prefers to live in the middle of its range
and is stressed by being run fully empty or kept fully full.
Modern lithium cells last longest with frequent partial
charges and a cool temperature, the opposite of the old rule.

A third error is underestimating heat. Temperature is the
great enemy of battery life and safety. Heat accelerates the
wear reactions, so a battery kept hot ages far faster, and
extreme heat is what triggers the dangerous runaway. Leaving a
device or an electric car battery baking in the sun is quietly
shortening its life, and charging a very cold lithium battery
can damage it too. Much of caring for batteries is simply
managing their temperature.

A fourth misconception is that voltage tells you how much
energy is left. Voltage sags only modestly as many batteries
discharge, so it is a poor fuel gauge; what matters is
capacity, how much charge the cell can still deliver, which is
why devices estimate remaining energy with more than a simple
voltage reading. Confusing voltage with charge leads people to
misjudge both how full a battery is and why two batteries of
the same voltage can hold wildly different amounts of energy.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

Batteries connect directly to electricity and circuits, since
a battery is just a source of voltage driving current around a
loop, and understanding one illuminates the other. The same
ideas of voltage as a push and current as a flow describe both
the battery's internal chemistry and the external device it
powers.

They connect to the chemistry of oxidation and reduction,
which governs not only batteries but rusting, tarnishing,
electroplating, and the slow corrosion that eats metal
structures, all of which are the same electron-transfer
process in different dress. They connect to the challenge of
renewable energy and the grid, where storing power to match
supply with demand is the defining problem, and to the physics
of energy itself, since a battery is one more example of
energy changing form, from chemical to electrical to light,
heat, or motion, obeying the same conservation laws that run
every engine and every living cell. And they connect, through
thermal runaway, to the study of self-reinforcing feedback
loops, where a process that feeds on its own output can
accelerate out of control, a pattern that appears in systems
far beyond chemistry.
