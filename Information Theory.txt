INFORMATION THEORY

Information theory is the mathematics of what a
message actually carries. Not what it means, not
whether it is true, but how much of it there is,
how tightly it can be packed, and how much of it
survives a noisy wire. It gives a hard number to a
thing that sounds unmeasurable, the amount of
information in a signal, and from that one number
it derives firm limits on compression and
communication that no clever engineer can ever
beat.

It is one of the rare ideas with a clean birthday.
In 1948 a mathematician at Bell Labs named Claude
Shannon published a paper called "A Mathematical
Theory of Communication," and a whole field sprang
into existence nearly complete. Almost every
digital thing you touch, the fact that a photo
compresses, that a call survives a weak signal,
that a scratched disc still plays, that a password
can be strong, rests on what Shannon wrote down.

This booklet walks the shape of it. By the end you
should know what information means as a quantity,
why that definition was revolutionary, the engine
that drives compression and error correction, the
traps people fall into, and how the idea reaches
into physics, cryptography, biology, and machine
learning. Read it in pieces. Each section stands
on its own.

WHAT IT IS

Shannon's first move was to throw away meaning.
This sounds reckless and was exactly the point. To
an engineer building a telephone network, it does
not matter whether the wire carries a love letter
or a stock quote or pure nonsense. The job is to
reproduce at one end a message chosen at the other.
So Shannon defined information in a way that
ignores content entirely and looks only at one
thing: how surprising the message is.

Here is the core intuition. Information is the
resolution of uncertainty. If I tell you something
you already knew for certain, I have given you no
information at all. If I tell you the outcome of a
coin flip you could not have predicted, I have
given you some. The more surprising the news, the
more information it carries. A message that was one
of a thousand equally likely possibilities tells
you more than a message that was one of two.

Shannon made this exact. The amount of information
in an outcome is tied to how unlikely it was. A
rare event, when it happens, carries a lot of
information; a near-certain event carries almost
none. The unit is the BIT, short for binary digit,
and one bit is the information in a single
yes-or-no answer to a question where both answers
were equally likely. One fair coin flip is one bit.
Two flips, two bits. The outcome of rolling a fair
eight-sided die is three bits, because it takes
three yes-or-no questions to pin down one of eight
equal choices.

The central quantity of the whole field is called
ENTROPY, written H. The entropy of a source is the
average information per message it produces, the
average surprise, measured in bits. A source that
always sends the same symbol has entropy zero:
nothing is ever in doubt, nothing is learned. A
source that picks uniformly among many symbols has
high entropy: every message is a fresh surprise.
Entropy is a measure of uncertainty, and because
information is the removal of uncertainty, the two
are the same number seen from opposite sides.

The game of twenty questions is the cleanest way to
feel this. Each yes-or-no question, asked well, splits
the remaining possibilities in half, and each answer is
worth one bit. If there are a million equally likely
answers, you can corner the right one in about twenty
questions, because twenty halvings cut a million down to
one. That is why the information in a choice among a
million is about twenty bits: it is exactly the number
of good yes-or-no questions needed to find it. Entropy
is the average length of the shortest game of twenty
questions for a given source, and compression is just
the trick of writing down the answers to that game
instead of the message itself.

WHY IT MATTERS

Before Shannon, communication was a craft of rules
of thumb. Engineers knew that louder signals and
cleaner wires helped, and that noise was the enemy,
but no one could say how much information a channel
could carry, or how small a file could be squeezed,
or whether noise set a wall you simply could not
pass. These felt like engineering problems with no
final answers, only better and better hacks.

Shannon did not arrive from nowhere. Earlier Bell Labs
engineers, Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley, had taken
the first steps in the 1920s, groping toward a measure
of how fast a line could carry signals and sensing that
information ought to be countable. And Shannon himself
had already changed the world once: his 1937 master's
thesis showed that the true-or-false logic of Boolean
algebra could be built directly out of electrical
switches, the insight that underlies every digital
circuit ever since. He came to communication having
already fused logic and electricity, primed to see
messages as something that could be measured and
engineered rather than merely carried.

Shannon turned them into problems with exact
answers. He proved two results that bracket the
entire enterprise of digital communication. The
first, the source coding theorem, says there is a
hard floor to compression: a source cannot be
squeezed below its entropy without losing
information, and it can be squeezed arbitrarily
close to that floor. The second, the noisy channel
coding theorem, says every channel has a fixed
CAPACITY, a maximum rate of reliable information,
and that below that rate you can drive your error
down to nearly zero, while above it you cannot
communicate reliably at all.

That second result was the shocking one. The
intuition of the day was that noise forced a
tradeoff: to get fewer errors you had to slow down
or shout louder, and perfect reliability was
impossible over an imperfect wire. Shannon proved
the opposite. As long as you stay under the
capacity, you can make errors as rare as you like,
not by shouting but by clever coding. This is the
reason a space probe billions of miles away, with a
signal fainter than a whisper, can still send back
flawless pictures. It is operating under the
capacity of its channel, and theory guarantees that
nearly perfect reception is possible.

The payoff is everywhere in the modern world.
Compression formats for images, audio, and video
all chase the entropy floor. Error-correcting codes
in storage and transmission all exploit the
capacity theorem. Cryptography borrows entropy to
measure how unguessable a key is. The theory does
not just describe these technologies; it sets the
boundaries they live inside, telling engineers both
what is achievable and what is forever out of
reach, so no one wastes a career chasing a
compressor that beats entropy or a modem that beats
capacity. Knowing the wall is where the engineering
begins.

Part of why the theory spread so far is that it does
not care what the message is made of. A symbol can be
a letter, a dot of Morse, a pixel, a musical sample, a
base of DNA, a pulse of light in a fiber; the
mathematics is identical, because it only ever looks at
probabilities. This substrate independence meant the
same equations that sized a telephone line could size a
hard drive, a radio link, a compression format, and
later a gene. Shannon built one abstract picture, a
source sending symbols down a channel to a receiver,
and almost everything that moves or stores information
turned out to fit inside it.

THE CORE MECHANISM

The engine of information theory is the entropy
formula, and it is worth seeing plainly. For a
source that emits symbols, each symbol i appearing
with probability p, the entropy is the sum, over
all symbols, of the probability times the
surprise of that symbol. Surprise is measured as
the logarithm, base two, of one over the
probability. In compact form:

=====
    H = - sum over i of p_i * log2(p_i)
=====

Read it piece by piece. The term log2(1/p) is the
surprise of one symbol: a symbol with probability
one half scores log2(2) = 1 bit, a symbol with
probability one in eight scores log2(8) = 3 bits,
a certain symbol with probability one scores
log2(1) = 0 bits. Multiply each symbol's surprise
by how often it occurs, add them up, and you get
the average surprise per symbol. That average is
the entropy, and it is the true information content
of the source in bits per symbol.

A fair coin: two outcomes, each probability one
half, each surprise one bit, entropy one bit per
flip. A bent coin that lands heads ninety percent
of the time has lower entropy, about half a bit,
because most flips tell you little you did not
expect. Push it to a coin that always lands heads
and entropy falls to zero. Uncertainty and
information rise and fall together.

A worked example makes it concrete. Suppose a weather
station in a dull climate reports one of four states
each day: sunny half the time, cloudy a quarter of the
time, rainy an eighth, and stormy an eighth. A lazy
engineer assigns each state a two-bit code, since four
states fit in two bits, and spends two bits a day. But
the entropy of this source is lower. Sunny, at
probability one half, carries one bit; cloudy, at a
quarter, two bits; rainy and stormy, at an eighth each,
three bits. Weight each by how often it occurs and the
average comes to one and three-quarter bits per day.
That is the true information content, and it means a
smarter code can report the weather in less space than
the obvious two-bit scheme, every day, forever.

Behind that saving lies one of Shannon's most elegant
insights, the idea of TYPICAL sequences. Over a long
run, the messages a source actually produces are not
spread evenly across all possible messages; they cluster
into a relatively small set of typical ones, sequences
whose mix of symbols matches the source's probabilities.
A long English text will have about the usual proportion
of E's and almost no strings of ten Z's. There are far
fewer typical sequences than possible ones, and since
only the typical ones show up in practice, you only need
enough codes to label those. Counting them gives exactly
the entropy. Compression works because most of what
could be said never is.

Now the first great consequence: COMPRESSION. If a
source has entropy of, say, two bits per symbol,
then on average you need at least two bits to encode
each symbol, and you can get as close to two as you
please. The trick is to stop spending the same
number of bits on every symbol. Common symbols
should get short codes, rare symbols long ones. This
is exactly how Morse code works, assigning a single
dot to the common letter E and longer sequences to
rare letters like Q, and it is the heart of HUFFMAN
CODING, an algorithm that builds the optimal set of
such variable-length codes from the symbol
frequencies.

The one rule such a code must obey is to be
prefix-free: no code is the beginning of another
code, so a receiver reading a stream of bits always
knows where one symbol ends and the next begins,
with no separators needed. A tiny example maps four
symbols to codes of unequal length:

=====
    A  ->  0
    B  ->  10
    C  ->  110
    D  ->  111
=====

The string 0 10 0 110 decodes unambiguously as
A B A C, read left to right, because the moment you
have read a complete code you commit to it; no valid
code starts with 0 except A, so the first 0 must be
A, and so on. If A is the most common symbol it now
costs a single bit, and the whole message shrinks
toward the entropy floor. Real compressors like the
ones behind ZIP files combine this idea with a
second one, spotting repeated patterns. That second
idea, the family of methods named after Abraham Lempel
and Jacob Ziv, works by building a dictionary on the
fly: the first time it sees a chunk of data it writes
it out, and every later time it sees the same chunk it
writes a short reference back to the first. Text full of
repeated words, a document, a web page, a log file,
collapses as each repeat becomes a pointer. Combine
dictionary references for repetition with Huffman codes
for the leftovers and you have, in essence, the engine
inside almost every ZIP file and web page sent to your
browser. Yet the Shannon limit stands over all of them:
no lossless method, however clever, can on average beat
the entropy of the source.

There is a second kind of compression that breaks this
rule on purpose. The entropy floor is absolute only for
LOSSLESS compression, where every bit of the original
must come back exactly. But for photos, music, and
video, nobody needs the exact original; they need
something that looks or sounds the same to a human. So
LOSSY compressors like the ones behind JPEG images and
MP3 audio throw away information the senses will not
miss, the subtle color shifts the eye ignores, the
faint sounds the ear cannot hear under louder ones, and
only then compress what remains. This is why a photo
can shrink to a tiny fraction of its size while still
looking fine. The theory that governs the tradeoff,
how much quality you lose for how many bits you save, is
called rate-distortion theory, and it is Shannon's as
well. Lossless coding asks what is the least you can
spend to keep everything; lossy coding asks what is the
least you can spend to keep what matters.

Now the second great consequence: fighting NOISE.
A channel corrupts some of the bits you send, a
flipped 0 here, a lost 1 there. The naive fix is
repetition: send every bit three times and let the
receiver take a majority vote. Send 1 as 111; if it
arrives as 101, two out of three say 1, so decode 1.
This works but is wasteful, tripling the traffic to
survive a little noise.

Shannon's capacity theorem promised something far
better: structured redundancy that corrects errors
at a tiny fraction of that cost. The workhorse idea
is the PARITY bit, a single extra bit that records
whether the number of 1s in a block is even or odd,
computed by exclusive-or across the data bits:

=====
    p = a ^ b ^ c
=====

The receiver recomputes p from the bits it got and
compares. If they disagree, an error occurred. A
single parity bit only detects one error; it cannot
say which bit flipped. But add a few parity bits,
each covering a cleverly chosen overlapping subset
of the data, and the pattern of which parity checks
fail points straight at the guilty bit, which you
then flip back. This is the HAMMING CODE, invented
by Richard Hamming at Bell Labs around the same
years: with just three parity bits guarding four
data bits, it corrects any single-bit error in the
block. Descendants of this idea, like Reed-Solomon
codes, are what let a scratched CD or a worn hard
drive hand back perfect data.

What sets the capacity in the first place? For the
common case of a signal fighting against background
noise, Shannon gave a formula, often called the
Shannon-Hartley theorem, that pins it down. Capacity
rises with two things: the BANDWIDTH of the channel,
how wide a range of frequencies it can use, and the
signal-to-noise ratio, how loud the signal is compared
to the noise. Crucially, capacity grows only slowly,
logarithmically, with signal strength: to double your
reliable rate by shouting alone you must raise the
signal power far more than double. This is why wider
bandwidth, more spectrum, is so prized, and why the
engineering of radio and fiber is a constant fight for
capacity measured in these exact terms. The abstract
bits-per-second limit becomes a concrete budget an
engineer can read off a datasheet.

Notice the deep symmetry. Compression REMOVES
redundancy to get near the entropy floor.
Error correction ADDS redundancy, carefully
shaped, to climb toward the channel capacity. The
same theory governs both directions: strip out the
predictable to save space, put back the structured
and predictable to survive noise. Information lives
in the gap between those two moves.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

The first and deepest confusion is between
information and meaning. In Shannon's theory a page
of random letters carries MORE information than a
page of ordinary English, because it is more
surprising, less predictable. This feels backward
to anyone who thinks information means useful
content. But Shannon's information is a measure of
surprise and unpredictability, not of value or
truth. A completely random signal has maximum
entropy and is also completely useless, which tells
you that information in this technical sense is not
the same as knowledge. Keep the two apart or nothing
in the field makes sense.

The second error is thinking that more bits always
means more information. The whole art of
compression is proof otherwise. An uncompressed
photo and its compressed version carry the same
information in far fewer bits, because the original
was padded with redundancy, with predictable
patterns that cost space but added nothing. Bits are
the container; information is what is actually inside.
A big file can be nearly empty of information, and a
small one can be packed to the brim.

The third error treats entropy as a bad thing, a
synonym for disorder and decay. In information
theory high entropy simply means high uncertainty,
which is exactly what you want from, for example, a
cryptographic key: a key with low entropy is
guessable and weak, a key with high entropy is
strong. The same quantity that spells doom in one
story spells security in another. Entropy is neutral;
it measures uncertainty, and whether that is good or
bad depends entirely on what you are trying to do.

The fourth error is believing the capacity theorem
hands you the codes. Shannon proved that good codes
EXIST, that reliable communication below capacity is
possible, but his proof was not a recipe. It showed
the destination without drawing the road. Finding
actual codes that approach capacity took another half
century of work, and the codes that finally got close,
in the 1990s and 2000s, powered the mobile phones and
internet links of today. A proof that something is
possible is not the same as knowing how, a gap worth
remembering well beyond this field.

A fifth error is the dream of endless compression: if a
zip file is smaller, why not zip the zip, and zip that,
squeezing any file down to a single bit? Entropy forbids
it, and the reason is a clean counting argument. A good
compressor has already wrung the redundancy out, so its
output looks nearly random, with entropy packed close to
the maximum, and random-looking data cannot be
compressed further. More generally, no method can shrink
every possible input, because there are simply not enough
short outputs to go around: if some files get shorter,
others must get longer, and a scheme that always wins is
as impossible as a perpetual motion machine. Compression
is not magic subtraction; it is the removal of a finite
amount of redundancy that, once gone, is gone.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

The nearest and strangest connection is to PHYSICS.
Shannon's entropy formula is, apart from a constant,
identical to the formula for entropy in
thermodynamics, the quantity that measures disorder
in a gas and dictates that heat flows from hot to
cold. This was not a coincidence Shannon forced; the
mathematician John von Neumann reportedly urged him to
use the word entropy precisely because the same
mathematics already ruled physics. The link runs deep.
Erasing one bit of information must, by a result known
as Landauer's principle, release a tiny minimum of
heat, binding the abstract accounting of information to
the physical accounting of energy. Information, it
turns out, is not free-floating; it is physical.

This link resolved a puzzle that had haunted physics for
nearly a century, the paradox of MAXWELL'S DEMON. In the
1860s James Clerk Maxwell imagined a tiny being guarding
a door between two boxes of gas, letting fast molecules
through one way and slow ones the other, sorting hot from
cold without spending energy and so, apparently, breaking
the law that disorder must increase. For generations no
one could say exactly why the demon fails. The answer,
sharpened by information theory, is that the demon must
MEASURE and REMEMBER which molecules are which, and that
information is physical: eventually its memory fills and
must be erased, and erasing it releases exactly the heat
the sorting seemed to save. The books balance only when
information is counted as a physical quantity on the
ledger. Shannon's bits turned out to be the missing term
in a famous law of nature.

The connection to PROBABILITY and inference is just
as tight, and it is where this booklet meets Bayesian
reasoning. Surprise is defined through probability, so
information theory and probability are two views of the
same structure. A key quantity, MUTUAL INFORMATION,
measures how much knowing one thing reduces your
uncertainty about another, how many bits of surprise
one variable removes from the other. That is a precise,
general measure of dependence, and it underlies modern
statistics and much of machine learning, where models
are trained by minimizing a quantity, cross-entropy,
that is pure information theory: the extra bits you
waste when your predicted probabilities differ from
reality.

CRYPTOGRAPHY grew up alongside the theory, and Shannon
himself wrote its foundational secret paper. Entropy
measures how unguessable a key or a password is: a
truly random 128-bit key has 128 bits of entropy and
cannot be brute-forced; a password built from a common
word has far less, however long it looks. Shannon even
proved that one encryption scheme, the one-time pad, is
perfectly unbreakable, precisely because the ciphertext
carries zero information about the message to anyone
without the key. Secrecy, in this light, is the art of
making your message look like maximum entropy to an
eavesdropper.

The reach extends further still. In BIOLOGY, DNA is a
code over four symbols, and information theory supplies
the natural language for how genetic messages are
stored, copied, and corrupted by mutation. In
LINGUISTICS, it quantifies the redundancy of human
languages: English runs roughly seventy-five percent
redundant, which is why you can rd a sentnce wth
missng lttrs, the predictability that compression
exploits and that also makes language robust against
noise. And in the theory of computation it meets its
cousin, KOLMOGOROV COMPLEXITY, which measures the
information in a single object as the length of the
shortest program that could generate it, tying
Shannon's statistical entropy to the limits of
computation itself.

It is worth pausing on how invisibly the theory runs
the machinery of ordinary life. When a spacecraft at
the edge of the solar system sends home a photograph,
its signal is almost drowned in noise, yet
error-correcting codes rebuild it bit-perfect; the
Voyager probes have done this for decades from billions
of miles away. The little QR code on a package can be
scanned even when smudged or torn, because roughly a
third of it is error-correcting redundancy by design.
Every phone call, every video stream, every file read
back from a solid-state drive passes through layers of
compression to save space and coding to survive errors,
each layer sized by Shannon's two limits. The theory is
not a museum piece. It is the silent arithmetic under
the whole digital day, deciding how small things can get
and how reliably they can arrive.

That is the shape of information theory. One daring
decision, to measure messages by their surprise and
ignore their meaning, turned communication from a
craft into a science with laws as firm as physics.
Information became a quantity you could count in bits,
compression and error correction became two directions
of a single accounting, and a wall appeared around
every channel marking the exact border of the possible.
Everything digital was built in the space that one
1948 paper mapped out, and the map has not needed
redrawing since.
