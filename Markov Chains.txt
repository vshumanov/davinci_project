MARKOV CHAINS

WHAT IT IS

A Markov chain is a model of a system that moves between
states at random, where the next state depends only on the
current one and not on the whole history of how it got there.
This is the defining property, and it has a name:
memorylessness. The system does not remember its past.
Wherever it is now contains everything that matters for
predicting where it goes next. The weather tomorrow modeled as
depending only on the weather today, a board game piece whose
next square depends only on its current square and a die roll,
a web surfer whose next click depends only on the page they
are on: all are Markov chains.

The pieces are simple. There is a set of states the system can
be in. There are transition probabilities, the chances of
moving from each state to each other state in one step. That
is the entire description. To run the chain you start
somewhere and repeatedly roll the weighted dice that the
transition probabilities define, hopping from state to state.
The memoryless assumption is what makes the whole thing
tractable, because you never have to track history; you only
ever need to know the present state and the fixed table of
transition probabilities.

The idea comes from the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov,
who introduced it around 1906 and, in a famous demonstration,
applied it to a literary text, analyzing the sequence of
vowels and consonants in a Russian poem to show that his
methods worked even when successive events were dependent, not
independent. That was the point he was making: probability
theory had mostly assumed independent events, like separate
coin flips, and Markov showed how to handle chains of events
where each one is linked to the one before, which describes
far more of the real world.

WHY IT MATTERS

Markov chains matter because an enormous range of real
processes have, at least approximately, the memoryless
structure, and for those processes the model is both simple
and powerful. They describe queues, the spread of disease, the
behavior of molecules, the fluctuation of stock prices, the
wear of machinery, the sequence of words in text, and the
movement of populations. Whenever a system shuffles among a
set of conditions with roughly fixed step-to-step
probabilities, a Markov chain captures its behavior and lets
you compute things that would otherwise be hopeless.

They matter because they power technologies you use
constantly. The original Google search ranking, PageRank,
models a random web surfer clicking links as a Markov chain
and ranks pages by how often that surfer lands on them. The
predictive text and autocomplete that finish your sentences
grew from Markov models of which word tends to follow which.
Speech recognition, gene sequence analysis, and countless
simulations rest on Markov foundations. The modern methods
that sample from fiendishly complicated probability
distributions, essential to statistics and physics, are built
on cleverly designed Markov chains that wander through
possibilities and settle into the right proportions.

They matter, finally, because they teach a profound and
liberating simplification: that you often do not need the full
history to predict the future, only the present state, if you
choose your states well. This is both a practical tool and a
way of thinking. Framing a messy, history-dependent situation
as movement among a well-chosen set of states, with fixed
transition odds, can turn an intractable mess into something
you can calculate, and the art lies in choosing states that
make the memoryless assumption true enough to be useful.

THE MEMORYLESS PROPERTY

The heart of the matter deserves care, because it is subtle
and easy to get wrong. Memorylessness does not mean the future
is independent of the past. It means the present state
summarizes everything about the past that is relevant to the
future. If you know the current state exactly, then knowing
additionally how you arrived tells you nothing more about
where you will go. All the influence of history is already
baked into where you are now.

This is less restrictive than it first sounds, because you get
to define what counts as a state. If the next outcome seems to
depend on the last two steps, you can make the state the pair
of last two steps, and the process becomes memoryless again at
that level. A weather model where tomorrow depends on today
and yesterday is not memoryless in terms of single days, but
it is a Markov chain if each state is a pair of consecutive
days. By folding enough history into the definition of the
state, a great many processes can be made Markov, which is why
the framework is so widely applicable. The skill is packing
just enough into the state to capture the real dependence
without making the state space hopelessly large.

STATES, TRANSITIONS, AND THE STATIONARY DISTRIBUTION

Picture a tiny weather chain with two states, Sunny and Rainy,
and these rules: a sunny day is followed by another sunny day
with probability nine in ten and by rain one in ten; a rainy
day is followed by sun half the time and rain half the time.
That table of four numbers is the whole model.

=====
    from \ to    Sunny   Rainy
    Sunny         0.9     0.1
    Rainy         0.5     0.5
=====

Now ask a natural question: over the long run, what fraction
of days are sunny? You could simulate the chain for thousands
of steps and count, but Markov theory gives the answer
directly. As the chain runs for a long time, under mild
conditions it forgets where it started and settles into a
stable mix of states called the stationary distribution, the
proportions that no longer change from step to step. For this
weather chain, the long-run fraction of sunny days works out
to about five in six. Start sunny or start rainy; run it long
enough and you converge to the same steady climate.

The stationary distribution is often the real prize. It
answers questions like what fraction of time a machine spends
broken, what share of web traffic lands on each page, or what
proportion of a population ends up in each category, all
without tracing any individual path. It is found by solving
for the distribution that maps to itself under one step of the
chain: feed it in, and the same proportions come out. This
self-consistency, the distribution that is unchanged by the
chain's own dynamics, is exactly what makes it stationary, and
it is the mathematical sibling of equilibrium in physics and
of the steady state in engineering.

PAGERANK AS A RANDOM WALK

The clearest grand example is PageRank, the algorithm that
launched Google. Imagine a surfer who starts on some web page
and, at each step, clicks a random link on the current page,
moving to wherever it leads. This is a Markov chain whose
states are web pages and whose transitions are the links. Its
stationary distribution says how often, in the long run, the
random surfer lands on each page, and a page the surfer visits
often is, by this measure, important. PageRank ranks pages by
exactly this stationary probability.

There is one wrinkle that reveals a general lesson. A pure
link-following surfer can get stuck: a page with no outgoing
links traps them, and clusters of pages that only link among
themselves hoard the surfer. The fix is to let the surfer,
with some small probability at each step, ignore the links and
jump to a completely random page, as if getting bored and
typing a new address. This teleportation keeps the chain
connected and well behaved, guaranteeing that a single stable
stationary distribution exists and can be found. The trick of
adding a little random restart to keep a chain healthy is a
widely reused idea, and it shows how a small structural
adjustment can rescue the mathematical guarantees a Markov
chain needs.

HIDDEN CHAINS AND SAMPLING

Two powerful extensions turn the basic chain into some of the
most useful machinery in science and technology. The first is
the hidden Markov model. In many situations the states
themselves are hidden from you, and all you observe are noisy
outputs that each state tends to produce. In speech, the
hidden states might be the sounds a speaker intends, while the
observations are the messy acoustic signal; in biology, the
hidden states might be regions of a gene, while the
observations are the letters of the DNA. A hidden Markov model
lets you infer the most likely hidden sequence from the
visible outputs, and an elegant procedure called the Viterbi
algorithm finds that best path efficiently. This single idea
underpinned decades of speech recognition and remains central
to analyzing sequences of every kind.

The second extension runs the logic backward. Normally you
have a chain and want its stationary distribution; Markov
chain Monte Carlo flips this, starting with a distribution you
wish you could sample from but cannot, often a fiendishly
complicated one from statistics or physics, and cleverly
designing a chain whose stationary distribution is exactly
that target. You then simply let the chain wander, and after
it settles, the states it visits are samples from the
distribution you wanted. This trick made it possible to do
Bayesian statistics and to simulate physical systems that were
previously intractable, and it is one of the most
consequential computational methods of the last century, all
resting on the humble fact that a well-built chain forgets
where it started and converges to a known mix.

Both extensions depend on a quantity the basic story glosses
over: the mixing time, how long the chain must run before it
has genuinely forgotten its starting point and reached its
stationary distribution. A chain can have a perfectly
well-defined long-run behavior yet take an enormous number of
steps to get there, and in sampling methods this is the whole
practical difficulty, because samples taken before the chain
has mixed are biased by where you began. Judging when a chain
has truly settled is subtle and sometimes impossible to
guarantee, which is why mixing time, invisible in the tidy
theory, dominates the messy practice.

Not every chain wanders forever; some have states you can
enter but never leave, called absorbing states, and these
model processes that end. The classic example is the gambler's
ruin: a gambler bets repeatedly until they either hit a target
or go broke, and broke and target are both absorbing, states
the chain stops at. For such chains the interesting questions
are not long-run proportions but which absorbing state you end
in and how long you take to get there. The mathematics answers
both, giving, for instance, the probability a gambler is
ruined before reaching their goal and the expected number of
bets until the game ends. The same structure models how long a
machine runs before failure, how a disease progresses to
recovery or death, and how a random process reaches any final,
sticky condition.

A last idea explains why the stationary distribution is so
often reachable and so often unique. A chain that can
eventually get from any state to any other, and that does not
march through its states in a rigid repeating cycle, is called
ergodic, and ergodic chains are guaranteed to converge to a
single stationary distribution regardless of where they start.
Many well-behaved chains also satisfy a stronger, tidier
condition called detailed balance, in which the flow from each
state to each other is matched by an equal flow back, so the
whole system is in a kind of microscopic equilibrium. Detailed
balance is the property that physicists exploit when they
design chains to sample from a target distribution, because a
chain built to satisfy it is guaranteed to settle into exactly
the distribution they want. These conditions are the fine
print that separates the chains whose beautiful long-run
behavior can be trusted from those where it cannot be. They
are easy to overlook precisely because the headline result,
convergence to a single stationary distribution, is so clean
and so convenient, but a chain that quietly violates them will
mislead anyone who assumes the tidy theory simply applies.
Checking that a chain can reach everywhere from anywhere, and
that it does not march through its states in a rigid cycle, is
the small act of diligence that keeps the powerful machinery
honest.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

The first misconception is that memorylessness means history
does not matter or that the process is purely random noise.
History matters enormously; it is simply all summarized in the
current state. A Markov chain can have rich, structured,
highly predictable behavior. Memoryless is a statement about
where the relevant information lives, in the present rather
than spread across the past, not a claim that the past had no
effect.

A second error is forcing the Markov assumption onto processes
that genuinely depend on long, detailed history in ways no
reasonable state can capture. If the next outcome truly
depends on a long and specific sequence of past events, a
simple Markov model will fit poorly, and no modest enlargement
of the state will save it. The framework is powerful but not
universal, and part of using it well is recognizing when the
memoryless assumption is a reasonable approximation and when
it is a distortion.

A third error is assuming every chain settles into a unique
stationary distribution. Chains that are disconnected, that
split into regions the system cannot move between, or that
cycle rigidly through states in lockstep may have several
stationary distributions or none that the chain actually
converges to. The convergence that makes the stationary
distribution so useful requires conditions, essentially that
the chain can eventually get from anywhere to anywhere and
does not march in a fixed cycle, and ignoring those conditions
leads to confident but wrong conclusions.

A fourth, subtle error is confusing the stationary
distribution with a prediction of the next step. The
stationary distribution describes long-run proportions, where
the system spends its time over many steps, not where it will
be tomorrow. In the short run the chain can be far from its
stationary mix, and how long it takes to settle, the mixing
time, is a separate and often difficult question that the
stationary distribution alone does not answer.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

Markov chains connect to information theory, since a source
that emits symbols with probabilities depending on the
previous symbol is a Markov source, and its entropy, the true
information rate, is computed from the chain's transition
structure. The redundancy that compression exploits and the
predictability that language models capture both live in these
transition probabilities.

They connect to queueing theory, whose standard models are
Markov chains over the number of items in the system, with
arrivals and departures as transitions, so that the
waiting-line results flow from Markov analysis. They connect
to game theory and decision-making through Markov decision
processes, which add choices and rewards to the states and
transitions and form the backbone of reinforcement learning,
the method behind much of modern artificial intelligence. And
they connect to the broad scientific habit of modeling change
as movement among states with fixed odds, a stance that turns
up in physics, chemistry, biology, and economics, and that
reflects a deep and repeated discovery: that many complicated
histories can be captured by a well-chosen present and a
simple rule for what comes next.
