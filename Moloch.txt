MOLOCH

Moloch is a name for a particular kind of disaster: the
one where everybody can see what is going wrong,
everybody would prefer it to stop, and it does not stop,
because no single person can stop it alone. It is the
logic behind arms races, polluted rivers, exhausting
workweeks, clickbait, and a hundred other situations
where rational individuals, each doing the sensible
thing for themselves, together produce an outcome that
none of them wanted. There is no villain. There is only
the trap.

The word is borrowed. Moloch was an ancient god to whom
people were said to sacrifice their own children, and
the name became a byword for a system that devours the
very things its worshippers love. It is used here as a
deliberate metaphor, a way of giving a face to something
that has no face: the blind, grinding pressure of bad
incentives. Naming it Moloch does not mean believing in
a demon. It means noticing that these failures behave as
if something were driving them, when in truth the driver
is just the structure of the situation.

This booklet lays out what Moloch is, why the idea is so
useful, the mechanism that powers these traps, the
mistakes people make in thinking about them, and how the
concept connects to game theory, economics, and
evolution. Read it in pieces. Each section stands on its
own.

WHAT IT IS

Moloch is the personification of COORDINATION FAILURE. A
coordination failure is a situation where a group of
people would all be better off if they could act
together in a certain way, but each individual, acting
alone, has no incentive to, and so the good outcome never
happens. Everyone is behaving rationally given their own
position, and the result is collectively terrible. The
tragedy is not that people are stupid or wicked. It is
that doing the right thing alone is punished, so no one
does it.

The image comes from the poet Allen Ginsberg, whose 1955
poem "Howl" contains a howling passage naming Moloch as
the thing that eats the young, grinds up human lives, and
runs the machinery of a soulless society. In 2014 the
writer Scott Alexander, in an essay called "Meditations
on Moloch," took Ginsberg's figure and turned it into a
sharp analytical tool. His framing question was simple
and devastating: if everyone hates the current system,
who or what keeps it going? The answer is Moloch, not as
a being but as the sum of all the incentives that make
each person keep feeding a machine they would rather see
destroyed.

The choice of Moloch as the name is not decoration; the old
god's defining horror is exactly right. Moloch was the deity
to whom worshippers were said to burn their own children,
sacrificing the thing they loved most and valued most for
the future, in exchange for some present advantage, victory,
favor, survival. That is precisely what a coordination trap
extracts: the competitors feed their deepest values,
including the long-term good of the very thing they are
competing over, into the fire for a short-term edge in the
race. The metaphor captures the particular tragedy that the
sacrifice is not of something worthless but of something
precious, and that it is made not by monsters but by ordinary
people who feel they have no choice. The horror is in the
willingness, born of competition, to give up what matters
most.

The key move is treating the SYSTEM as if it were an
agent with a will of its own, precisely in order to show
that it is not. When a factory pollutes a river that all
the factory owners also drink from, it is tempting to ask
who wanted this. Nobody did. But if cleaning up costs
money, any single owner who cleans up alone just goes
bankrupt and is replaced by someone who pollutes, so the
pollution continues as if some power demanded it. Calling
that power Moloch throws into relief how a result nobody
chose can be enforced on everybody by the shape of the
game they are all trapped in.

WHY IT MATTERS

The idea matters because it explains a feature of the
world that is otherwise baffling: the persistence of
problems that everyone agrees are problems. We are used
to thinking that if something is bad and widely disliked,
someone will fix it, or at least that it exists because
someone benefits from it. Moloch names the large class of
cases where neither is true, where a bad thing continues
not because anyone wants it but because the incentives
punish whoever tries to stop first.

Once you have the concept, you see it everywhere. Two
countries pour their wealth into weapons neither wants to
use, because the one that stops first is left
vulnerable. Companies cut corners and underpay because
the one that does not is undercut by the one that does.
Scientists chase flashy, publishable results instead of
careful truth, because careers are built on publication.
Social media rewards outrage because outrage spreads,
and any platform that refuses to amplify it loses to one
that will. In each case, the people inside can see the
damage and feel powerless, because they are: the pressure
is structural, and individual virtue cannot overcome it.

The reason this is worth understanding is that it changes
what you do about such problems. If you believe bad
systems are caused by bad people, you try to replace the
people, and nothing changes, because the new people face
the same incentives. If you understand Moloch, you see
that the fix is almost never willpower or good
intentions, but changing the STRUCTURE of the game so
that doing the right thing is no longer punished. That is
a completely different kind of project, and recognizing
which kind of problem you face is the first step to not
wasting your effort on the wrong one.

The concept also cuts across the usual political quarrels in
a clarifying way. One side tends to blame greedy
corporations, the other to blame corrupt governments, and
both are hunting for the villainous agent that Moloch says
often is not there. A Molochian analysis suggests that many
of the failures each side decries are not caused by bad
actors at all but by bad equilibria that would capture
whoever stood in those positions. This does not mean no one
is ever to blame, but it redirects attention from the
satisfying work of finding someone to punish toward the
harder, more useful work of asking what incentive structure
produced the behavior and how it could be changed. Blame is
emotionally gratifying and usually leaves the machine intact;
understanding the trap is the only thing that dismantles it.

THE CORE MECHANISM

The engine of Moloch is what Scott Alexander called a
MULTIPOLAR TRAP, and it has a precise shape worth taking
apart. The classic two-person version is the famous
PRISONER'S DILEMMA: two prisoners, each better off
betraying the other no matter what the other does, so
both betray, though both would have been better off
staying silent. Moloch is what you get when you run that
same logic across many players and many rounds, until it
becomes the permanent weather of a whole system.

Here is the general pattern. Imagine many actors
competing, each wanting to do well. There is some value
they all care about, clean water, honest reporting,
humane working hours, safety, truth. Sacrificing a little
of that value gives an actor a competitive edge: the
factory that pollutes is cheaper, the paper that
exaggerates gets more attention, the firm that overworks
its staff ships faster. As soon as one actor sacrifices
the value for an edge, the others must follow or lose.
Round after round, each actor trades away more of the
shared value just to keep up, until it is all gone, and
everyone is worse off than when they started, yet no one
can stop.

The cruel heart of it is that UNILATERAL restraint is
suicidal. Any single player who refuses to sacrifice the
value, who keeps the river clean or the workweek humane,
simply loses to those who do not, and is driven out,
replaced by someone who will race to the bottom with the
rest. So the good actors are not just outcompeted; they
are selected AGAINST and removed from the game. Over
time the system fills up with whoever is most willing to
sacrifice the shared value, and the behavior everyone
hates becomes mandatory just to survive.

The TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS is the cleanest real example.
Picture a shared pasture open to all herders. Each herder
gains fully from adding one more animal to his own herd,
but the cost of overgrazing is spread across everyone. So
each rationally adds animals, the pasture is destroyed by
overgrazing, and all the herders lose their livelihood. No
herder wanted the pasture ruined. Each merely followed his
own clear interest, and the sum of their sensible choices
was collective catastrophe. The same shape governs
overfished seas, crowded roads, and a warming atmosphere.

It is worth running the shape through a few more arenas,
because its reach is the whole point. In a competitive
market, any firm that refuses to cut a dangerous corner
its rivals are cutting loses on price and is driven out,
so safety and quality erode to whatever the law forces.
In science, any researcher who takes the slow, careful,
unglamorous path is out-published by those who chase
flashy, fragile results, so the careful are pushed out of
the field. In politics, any candidate who refuses to run
attack ads or take dark money is beaten by one who will.
In the attention economy, any publication that declines
to sensationalize is starved of clicks by one that does.
Even in personal life, if everyone in an industry answers
email at midnight, the one who logs off looks lazy and
falls behind. In every case the pattern is identical:
a value, safety, truth, honesty, rest, that each actor
would prefer to keep, competed away because keeping it
alone is a losing move.

So why has the world not already been ground entirely to
dust? Because several forces hold Moloch back, and seeing
them is the key to fighting it. Sometimes a physical LIMIT
stops the race: there are only so many hours in a day, so
much a body can take, so much a resource can yield, and the
ratchet jams against a hard wall. Sometimes there is SLACK,
an abundance of resources that means actors are not yet
desperate enough to sacrifice everything to compete. And
most importantly, sometimes there is enforced COORDINATION,
a rule, a law, a norm, a shared authority that binds all
the actors at once so that none can gain by defecting.
Every bit of the humane world, the weekends, the clean air,
the safety standards, the honest institutions, exists
because some coordinating force is actively holding a
particular Moloch at bay. Remove the force, and the race to
the bottom resumes.

Notice what the mechanism does NOT require. It needs no
malice, no stupidity, no conspiracy, and no coordination
among the actors at all. In fact it is powered by the
ABSENCE of coordination. Each actor may be intelligent,
well-meaning, and fully aware of the disaster unfolding,
and still be forced to contribute to it, because the
alternative is to lose and be replaced by someone who
contributes anyway. This is why Moloch feels like a
malevolent force. A result that requires no one's
intention and overrides everyone's preference genuinely
behaves like something with a will, even though it is only
the mathematics of the situation grinding forward.

And there is a ratchet. Once some actors have sacrificed a
value for an edge, the baseline shifts, and the new normal
becomes the floor from which the next round of sacrifice
begins. The pressure only pushes one way, toward trading
more and more of what everyone cares about for the thin,
temporary advantage of keeping pace. Left alone, a
multipolar trap tends to consume every value that can be
traded for competitive advantage, stopping only when there
is nothing left to give.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

The first and most common error is looking for someone to
blame. Faced with a Molochian disaster, people assume
there must be a villain, a greedy cabal, a corrupt elite,
someone who wants this and profits from it. Usually there
is not. The polluters would prefer clean water too; the
overworked and the overworking boss both hate the hours.
Moloch's signature is exactly that the harm persists with
no one willing it. Hunting for a culprit not only fails to
fix the problem, it misdirects the anger that might have
gone toward changing the structure.

The second error is believing the trap can be escaped by
individual virtue, by one person or firm or nation simply
choosing to do better. This is not just ineffective; it
can be counterproductive. The lone factory that cleans up
goes bankrupt and is replaced by a dirtier one, so the
river is no cleaner and a conscientious owner is gone.
Unilateral goodness inside a multipolar trap is punished
and selected out. This is a hard and unwelcome truth, but
missing it leads people to moralize at victims of the trap
as if they simply lacked the courage to be good.

The third error is the opposite despair: concluding that
since individual effort fails, nothing can be done.
Moloch is not invincible. What defeats a coordination trap
is COORDINATION, some mechanism that binds all the actors
at once so that doing the right thing is no longer a losing
move. A law that forces every factory to clean up removes
the penalty for cleaning up. A treaty that limits every
nation's weapons lets each disarm safely. The escape is
real, but it operates at the level of the whole system, not
the individual, which is why building and enforcing such
mechanisms is one of the central tasks of civilization.

The fourth error is thinking Moloch only concerns big,
obvious commons like fisheries and the climate. The same
logic runs through ordinary life: the party where everyone
stays late because no one wants to be the first to leave,
the group project where effort is shared but credit is
diluted, the neighborhood where everyone keeps their lawn
pristine because of what the neighbors will think. Once you
know the shape, you find it at every scale, from two people
to seven billion, wherever individual incentives and
collective good pull apart.

A fifth error, and a subtle one, is to take the whole idea
as an excuse, a way of shrugging that "it's just Moloch, the
system, nothing to be done." This gets the lesson exactly
backward. Naming the trap is not a counsel of despair but the
first move toward escape; you cannot build a coordination
mechanism against a force you have not identified. The point
of personifying the system as Moloch is to make visible a
problem that was invisible precisely because it had no agent
behind it, so that human agents can then organize against it.
Used as a shrug, the concept is worse than useless; used as a
diagnosis, it is the start of the cure.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

Moloch is the dramatized, generalized face of GAME THEORY,
the mathematical study of strategic choice. Its backbone is
the prisoner's dilemma and the idea of a NASH EQUILIBRIUM, a
situation where no player can improve their own outcome by
changing strategy alone, even when all players together could
do better by changing at once. A multipolar trap is simply a
bad Nash equilibrium that a whole society is stuck in. Moloch
gives that dry technical object an emotional weight and a
name, which is why the metaphor spread far beyond the people
who would ever read a game-theory textbook.

It connects directly to the economist's TRAGEDY OF THE
COMMONS, named in a famous 1968 essay by Garrett Hardin, and
to the long tradition of thinking about PUBLIC GOODS and
EXTERNALITIES, the costs and benefits that spill onto people
who did not choose them. Pollution is the textbook
externality, and it is textbook Moloch: a cost pushed onto
the shared world while the benefit is captured privately. The
whole apparatus of taxes, regulations, and property rights
that economists propose is, in this light, a toolkit for
escaping Molochian traps by realigning private incentives
with the common good.

The darkest connection is to EVOLUTION itself, which is
Moloch written into biology. Natural selection rewards
whatever spreads, not whatever is good for the species or the
world. A peacock's absurd tail, a costly advertisement that
helps no one, persists because peahens prefer it and any
plain-tailed male loses the mating race; the whole species is
stuck dragging heavy tails no one benefits from. Evolution is
full of such traps, races to the bottom that no creature
chose and none can leave. Life is shot through with the same
logic that ruins the commons.

The resolution has a name too, drawn from political
philosophy: the LEVIATHAN, the overarching power that can
bind everyone at once. Thomas Hobbes argued that people
escape the war of all against all only by handing authority
to a sovereign strong enough to enforce the rules on everyone
equally. In the language of this booklet, a Leviathan is a
Moloch-killer: a coordination mechanism, usually a government
or a binding agreement, that makes cooperation safe by
removing the penalty for going first. Every functioning law,
treaty, and institution is a small Leviathan raised against
some particular Moloch.

History offers real victories to prove the traps are
beatable. In the 1980s the world faced a classic multipolar
trap in the chemicals that were destroying the ozone layer:
no single country gained by banning them alone, since it
would bear the cost while others kept polluting the shared
sky. The escape was coordination, the Montreal Protocol, a
binding international agreement that committed everyone at
once, removing the penalty for going first; the ozone layer
is now slowly healing. Enforceable fishing quotas have
pulled some fisheries back from collapse the same way, and
labor laws ended the race to the bottom on child labor and
the hundred-hour week. These are Molochs that were named,
understood, and defeated by deliberately built coordination.
They are the proof that the grinding logic is not destiny,
only a default that enough collective will can override.

The idea has found new and urgent life in the debate over
ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE. Many worry that competition between
companies and nations to build ever more powerful systems is
itself a multipolar trap: each developer, fearing to fall
behind, races ahead and cuts corners on safety, so that even
developers who would prefer caution are forced to sprint. The
feared outcome is a world that races to build something
dangerous not because anyone wants danger, but because no one
can afford to slow down alone. It is Moloch in its newest and
perhaps most consequential costume.

Seen from far enough back, the whole history of CIVILIZATION
can be read as a long war against Moloch. Every institution
that works, the rule of law, the enforceable contract, the
regulatory agency, the international treaty, the social norm
that shames a defector, is a tool for solving some
coordination problem that individuals could not solve alone.
Progress, in this view, is not automatic; it is the hard-won
accumulation of mechanisms that let people cooperate where
raw incentives would have them race to the bottom. This is a
sobering lesson, because it means the humane order is not the
natural default that persists on its own, but a structure
that must be actively maintained against a constant downward
pressure. Let the coordinating institutions decay and the old
traps reopen on their own.

Technology sits on both sides of this war, which is what
makes the present moment so fraught. On one hand, new
technologies spawn new Molochs faster than ever, the
attention economy and the AI race being only the latest, by
creating new arenas of ruthless competition before any
coordinating rules exist. On the other hand, technology also
offers new tools for coordination, better ways to
communicate, verify, agree, and enforce, that could in
principle bind actors together and defuse traps that were
once hopeless. Whether the age ahead is one of worse Molochs
or better coordination is, in a real sense, the central open
question the concept leaves us with.

That is Moloch. Not a demon, but the exact and merciless logic
by which the uncoordinated pursuit of individual advantage can
devour the things everyone holds dear. It explains why bad
systems outlive everyone's wish to end them, why blaming
villains and summoning willpower so often fail, and why the
real cure is the hard, unglamorous work of coordination. Learn
to recognize the trap, and you stop being surprised that the
world is full of problems no one wants, and you start asking
the only question that helps: not who is to blame, but how the
game can be changed.
