VIRTUAL PRIVATE NETWORKS

A short field guide to what a VPN actually does, how the tunnel
works under the hood, and what it can and cannot protect. Read a
section, put the phone away, come back later. Each section
stands on its own.

WHAT A VPN IS

A VPN, a virtual private network, is an encrypted tunnel between
your device and a server somewhere else, through which your
internet traffic is routed. That sentence contains the whole
idea, so unpack it slowly.

Normally, when your device talks to the internet, it hands each
packet of data to your local network, which passes it to your
internet provider, which forwards it toward its destination.
Everyone along that path can see where the packet is going, and
unless the content is separately encrypted, what is in it. Your
provider knows every site you connect to. The coffee shop wi-fi
knows. Each relay in between handles your packets in the clear
addressing.

A VPN inserts a new first step. Before any packet leaves your
device, software wraps it in a layer of encryption and sends it
to one specific machine: the VPN server. Only after it reaches
that server is the original packet unwrapped and sent on to its
real destination. Replies come back the same way, encrypted from
the server to you. To everyone on the path between you and the
VPN server, your traffic is now an unreadable stream going to a
single address. To everyone on the far side, the traffic appears
to originate from the VPN server, not from you.

So a VPN does two concrete things, and it is worth fixing them
firmly in mind because almost every misunderstanding comes from
forgetting their limits.

First, it ENCRYPTS the link between your device and the VPN
server. The local network, the other people on the wi-fi, and
your internet provider can no longer see which sites you visit
or what you send. They see only that you are connected to a VPN,
and how much data is flowing.

Second, it CHANGES your apparent address. Websites and services
see the VPN server's IP address and its location, not yours. If
the server is in another country, you appear to be in that
country.

That is the entire function. Encrypt the first hop, and borrow
someone else's address. Everything a VPN is good for, and
everything it fails at, follows from those two facts.

The word private in the name is older than the consumer product
and points at the original use. Companies built VPNs so that an
employee working from home could reach internal systems,
databases, file shares, printers, as if their laptop were
physically plugged into the office network. The tunnel made a
remote machine behave like a local one. The consumer privacy VPN
is the same technology aimed at a different goal: not reaching a
private network, but hiding your traffic from the network you
are on.

WHY IT MATTERS

A VPN earns its place in a few specific situations, and
oversells itself in many others. Being clear about the real uses
is the best defense against the marketing.

The strongest case is the UNTRUSTED NETWORK. When you connect to
wi-fi you do not control, an airport, a hotel, a cafe, a
conference, you are trusting whoever runs it, and anyone else
connected to it, not to snoop. Most traffic today is already
protected by HTTPS, the lock in the browser, so the content of
your banking session is encrypted regardless. But the network
can still see which sites you visit, and a hostile network can
attempt tricks to downgrade or redirect your connection. A VPN
closes that gap by encrypting everything to a server you chose,
turning a sketchy network into a dumb pipe that carries your
sealed traffic and learns nothing.

The second real case is HIDING YOUR TRAFFIC FROM YOUR INTERNET
PROVIDER. Your provider sees every destination you connect to
and, in many countries, is permitted to log it, sell it, or hand
it over. A VPN moves that visibility: the provider now sees only
an encrypted stream to the VPN. This matters more in places
where providers are legally required to retain browsing records,
or where they inject ads and tracking into traffic.

The third case is CHANGING YOUR APPARENT LOCATION. This is
bandwidth for two different needs. One is access: reaching
content or services restricted to a region, or getting a usable
internet connection from inside a country that blocks large
parts of the web. The other is a modest privacy gain: a website
sees a shared VPN address used by thousands of people, which is
a weaker identifier than your own address, though as a later
section explains, far from anonymity.

The fourth case is the ORIGINAL one, reaching a private network
remotely. This is the corporate and self-hosting use. A remote
worker reaches office systems. A person on the road reaches the
services running on their home server. Here the VPN is not about
hiding, it is about secure access to something that is not meant
to be public at all.

Underneath all four is a single theme worth stating plainly: a
VPN relocates trust, it does not remove it. Without a VPN, you
implicitly trust your local network and your internet provider
to see your traffic responsibly. With a VPN, you stop trusting
them and start trusting the VPN company instead, because it now
sits in the exact position they used to, able to see your
unencrypted-to-them traffic as it leaves the tunnel. Whether
that is an improvement depends entirely on whether the VPN
company deserves more trust than the party it replaced. That is
not a technical question. It is a question about a company's
incentives, honesty, and legal exposure, and it is the hinge on
which the whole value of a consumer VPN turns.

HOW IT WORKS

This is the mechanism. Understand it and the rest of the subject
stops being marketing and starts being engineering. Three ideas
stack together: tunneling, encapsulation, and the cryptographic
handshake. Then a few protocols implement them.

Start with ENCAPSULATION, which is the physical trick behind
tunneling. A normal packet is like a postcard. It has a
destination address written on the outside, say a web server,
and a message. Anyone handling it reads both. A VPN takes that
whole postcard, message and address together, encrypts it into
unreadable noise, and places it inside a new envelope. On the
outside of the new envelope is a single address: the VPN server.
The handlers along the way, your router and your provider, see
only the outer envelope. They route it to the VPN server and
learn nothing about the postcard sealed inside.

Here is the shape of it. The inner packet is the real one; the
outer wrapper is what the network actually sees.

=====
    [ outer header: to VPN server ]
      [ encrypted blob ]
        [ inner header: to real site ]
        [ inner data: your request  ]
=====

When the sealed envelope reaches the VPN server, the server
decrypts the blob, recovers the original postcard, and drops it
into the normal mail, addressed to the real site, but now with
the server's own return address. The reply arrives at the
server, which seals it back up and sends it down the tunnel to
you. Your device decrypts it and hands the recovered reply to
the app that asked for it. The application never knows any of
this happened; it thinks it is talking to the internet directly.
That transparency is why a VPN protects everything on the device
at once, every app, not just the browser.

Now the hard part: how the two ends agree on a secret to encrypt
with, over a network where anyone might be listening. This is
the KEY EXCHANGE, or handshake, and it rests on a piece of
mathematics worth knowing in outline, because it appears
everywhere in security.

The problem seems impossible. You and the server have never met
and share no secret, yet you must agree on an encryption key
while a eavesdropper watches every message you exchange. The
solution, Diffie-Hellman key exchange, uses math that is easy to
do forward and effectively impossible to reverse. Each side
generates a private number and derives a public value from it.
They swap public values in the open. Then each side combines its
own private number with the other's public value, and through
the structure of the math, both arrive at the same shared
secret, a number the eavesdropper cannot compute from the two
public values alone. From that shared secret they derive the
keys used to encrypt the session. A rough analogy: two people
mix paint colors. Each keeps a secret shade, both start from a
common public color, each mixes in their secret and sends the
result across, then each adds their secret again to the color
they received. Both end with the identical final mix, while an
observer who saw only the two sent colors cannot separate out
the secret shades. The real version uses large numbers and
modular arithmetic rather than paint, but the shape is the same.

Alongside the key exchange sits AUTHENTICATION. The handshake
must also prove the server is the real VPN server and not an
impostor who stepped into the middle. This is done with keys the
two sides already hold, or with certificates, so that each can
verify it is talking to the party it intended. Key exchange
without authentication would be defeated by a middleman who runs
a separate handshake with each side; authentication is what
shuts that door.

Once a shared key exists and both sides are authenticated, the
actual DATA ENCRYPTION is the cheap, fast part. A symmetric
cipher, the same key to lock and unlock, scrambles every packet.
Modern ciphers also attach an authentication tag to each packet
so the far side can detect if even a single bit was altered in
transit. Tampered packets are dropped rather than trusted.

These pieces are assembled differently by different PROTOCOLS,
and the choice matters for speed, reliability, and trust.

WireGuard is the modern one and now the default on most serious
services. Its defining virtue is smallness: a few thousand lines
of code, against hundreds of thousands in older systems, which
means far less surface for bugs to hide in and far less to
audit. It uses one fixed, modern set of cryptographic building
blocks, with nothing to configure, on the principle that fewer
options means fewer ways to set it up wrong. The primitives, for
the curious, are ChaCha20 for encryption, Poly1305 for the
authentication tag, and Curve25519 for the key exchange. It
connects and reconnects fast, which is why it feels quicker and
survives switching between wi-fi and mobile data well.

OpenVPN is the older workhorse, built on the widely used OpenSSL
library. It is slower and heavier than WireGuard but
battle-tested and extremely flexible. It can run over two
different transport modes: a fast one, and a reliable one that
disguises itself as ordinary secure web traffic, which helps it
slip through restrictive firewalls that block everything else.
That disguise is one reason it survives in hostile network
conditions where newer protocols are blocked.

IKEv2, usually paired with the IPsec suite, is especially common
on phones. Its signature feature is graceful handling of a
changing connection: when your phone moves from wi-fi to
cellular, IKEv2 can keep the tunnel alive across the switch
instead of dropping it. That makes it a natural fit for mobile
devices that change networks constantly.

Two supporting mechanisms complete the picture, and both are
failure-safety features rather than parts of the tunnel itself.

The KILL SWITCH addresses a dangerous gap. If the VPN connection
drops, momentarily, on a reconnect, after a crash, your device
will by default fall back to its normal, unprotected connection,
leaking your real address and traffic without any warning. A
kill switch blocks all internet access the instant the tunnel
fails, so nothing leaves the device outside the VPN. For anyone
relying on a VPN for more than convenience, this is not
optional.

DNS LEAK protection addresses a subtler gap. Before your device
can connect to a site by name, it asks a DNS server to translate
that name into an address. If those lookups are sent outside the
tunnel to your provider's DNS server, as they are by default on
many systems, then your provider still sees the name of every
site you visit even while the rest of your traffic is hidden. A
proper VPN routes DNS lookups through the tunnel to its own
resolver, closing the leak. Many real-world VPN failures over
the years have been exactly this: the traffic was tunneled but
the name lookups quietly were not.

One more mechanism is worth knowing because it is a common
setting: SPLIT TUNNELING. By default a VPN captures all of a
device's traffic and sends it down the tunnel. Split tunneling
relaxes that, letting you choose which apps or destinations go
through the VPN and which use the normal connection directly.
The appeal is practical: route your browser through the VPN
while letting a video call or a local printer bypass it for
speed and reach. The cost is a wider attack surface and more
ways to leak, since anything outside the tunnel is exposed as
usual, and a misconfigured split has been the source of real
leaks where traffic meant to be protected quietly took the open
road. It is a convenience feature, not a security one, and worth
turning off when the point of the VPN is protection rather than
selective routing.

A word on TRANSPORT and overhead rounds out the picture. A
tunnel is not free: wrapping every packet in encryption and an
extra header adds a little size and a little delay, and your
traffic now takes a detour through the VPN server before
reaching its destination, so a distant server means a slower
connection. This is why server choice affects speed, and why the
lean design of a modern protocol matters. Most tunnels run over
the fast, connectionless style of network transport, which does
not wait for acknowledgements and so keeps latency low; the
trade is that it is easier for a hostile network to spot and
block. To survive in places that actively hunt and block VPN
traffic, some services add obfuscation, dressing the tunnel up
to look like ordinary secure web browsing so that a censor
cannot easily tell a VPN connection from a visit to a normal
website. Obfuscation costs some speed and is off by default on
most services, because most users do not face a network trying
to stop them.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

A VPN is one of the most oversold products in technology, and
the misunderstandings are not harmless, because they lead people
to feel protected while behaving carelessly.

The first and biggest mistake is BELIEVING A VPN MAKES YOU
ANONYMOUS. It does not. It hides your address from the sites you
visit and your traffic from your local network, and that is all.
It does nothing about the dozens of other ways you are
identified. The moment you log in to any account, you have
announced exactly who you are, VPN or not. Websites still read
cookies stored in your browser. And they still perform browser
fingerprinting, reading the particular combination of your
screen size, fonts, graphics hardware, and dozens of other
traits to recognize your device, a method that works no matter
what address you arrive from, because it never looks at your
address at all. A VPN changes the return address on the
envelope. It does not change your handwriting. Treating it as a
cloak of invisibility is how people relax every other precaution
and end up more exposed, not less.

The second mistake is FORGETTING THAT THE VPN COMPANY CAN SEE
EVERYTHING YOUR PROVIDER USED TO. The tunnel protects your
traffic from the network you are on, but it has to end
somewhere, and at that end the VPN server unwraps your traffic
to send it onward. The company running that server sits in
precisely the position your internet provider occupied, able to
observe your destinations and, for anything not independently
encrypted, your content. You have not removed the watcher. You
have chosen a new one. This is why the identity and honesty of
the provider is the entire game, and why a free VPN deserves
deep suspicion: running servers costs real money, and a company
giving the service away is very likely funding it by collecting
and selling the very data you installed it to protect.

That leads to the third mistake: TRUSTING NO-LOGS CLAIMS ON
FAITH. Nearly every VPN advertises that it keeps no logs of your
activity. The claim is easy to make and, left as a promise,
worth little. What gives it weight is evidence. Some providers
submit to independent audits in which outside firms inspect
their actual servers and configurations, not just their printed
policy, and the useful audits are recent ones, since a review
from years ago says nothing about the setup today. Stronger
still is architecture that makes logging difficult by design,
such as servers that run entirely from volatile memory with no
permanent disk, so that any data vanishes the moment a machine
is rebooted. The strongest evidence of all is the real world:
there have been cases where authorities seized a VPN provider's
servers or served legal demands and came away with nothing,
because there was genuinely nothing stored to take. A no-logs
claim backed by a seizure that produced no data is in a
different league from one backed only by a marketing page.

The fourth mistake is ASSUMING A VPN ENCRYPTS YOUR TRAFFIC ALL
THE WAY TO THE WEBSITE. It does not. The VPN encrypts the leg
from your device to the VPN server. From the server onward to
the actual website, your traffic travels under whatever
protection it already had. If the site uses HTTPS, that leg is
still encrypted, by the site, not by the VPN. If it does not,
that leg is in the clear, exactly as it would have been without
the VPN, only now starting from the server instead of from you.
The VPN secures a segment of the journey, not the whole of it.
Confusing the two leads people to think a VPN substitutes for
the lock in the browser. It does not; they protect different
legs, and you want both.

The fifth mistake is THINKING A VPN HIDES YOU FROM GOVERNMENTS
OR SERIOUS ADVERSARIES. Against the bulk, automated collection
that treats you as one undefended target among millions, a VPN
genuinely helps. Against an adversary specifically focused on
you, with legal power over the VPN company or the ability to
watch both ends of your connection, it offers much less. Someone
who can observe the traffic entering the VPN and the traffic
leaving it can often match them by timing and size alone, a
technique called traffic correlation, without ever breaking the
encryption. A VPN is a tool against routine surveillance and
casual snooping. It is not a shield against a motivated state,
and selling it as one is dangerous to the people who most need
to understand the difference.

The sixth mistake is CONFUSING THE VPN WITH THE PROXY IT
RESEMBLES. A simple proxy also changes your apparent address,
but typically for one app and often without real encryption. A
VPN encrypts and reroutes everything on the device at the
operating-system level. They look similar in the one visible
effect, a changed location, which is why cheap proxies are
sometimes marketed in VPN language. The encryption and the
device-wide scope are what make a VPN a security tool rather
than merely a location trick.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

A VPN sits in a neighborhood of related tools and ideas, and
knowing the neighbors is what lets you reason about when a VPN
is the right instrument and when something else is.

It connects first to HTTPS, the encryption already built into
the web, and the relationship is commonly misunderstood. HTTPS
encrypts the content of your connection from your browser all
the way to the website, including across the VPN server, which
is why even your VPN provider cannot read the inside of an HTTPS
session. A VPN encrypts everything leaving your device to the
VPN server, including which sites you are visiting, which HTTPS
alone reveals. They cover different things: HTTPS hides the
contents of each conversation end to end; the VPN hides the list
of who you are talking to from your local network and provider.
They are complementary, not alternatives, and the sensible setup
uses both.

It connects to TOR, which pursues the anonymity a VPN only
gestures at. Where a VPN routes your traffic through one
company's server, Tor routes it through three volunteer-run
relays in sequence, each peeling back one layer of encryption,
arranged so that no single relay knows both who you are and
where you are going. The entry relay sees your address but not
your destination; the exit relay sees your destination but not
your address. This buys far stronger anonymity than a VPN, at a
real cost in speed and convenience, and it removes the need to
trust any single operator. The trade is deliberate: a VPN is
fast and trusts one party; Tor is slow and trusts no single
party. For evading a serious adversary, Tor is the stronger
tool; for everyday privacy and unblocking, a VPN is the more
practical one.

It connects to the CORPORATE and self-hosting world, the
original purpose that the consumer product grew out of. In a
company, a VPN lets remote staff reach internal systems
securely, and for years it was the standard way to do so. That
model is now being questioned and partly replaced by an approach
called zero trust, which drops the assumption that anyone inside
the tunnel is automatically trusted and instead checks every
request to every system individually. For an individual, the
same technology running on a home server lets you reach your own
files and services from anywhere without exposing them to the
public internet, which is one of the cleanest and safest uses a
VPN has.

It connects to JURISDICTION and law, which quietly shapes how
much a VPN is worth. A VPN company is bound by the laws of the
country it operates in, including what data it can be compelled
to collect or surrender. The same no-logs promise means
something different depending on whether the company sits in a
country that can legally force secret data retention and gag the
company from admitting it. This is why the location of a
provider, and the legal regime it answers to, is a genuine part
of the privacy calculation and not mere marketing geography.

Finally, it connects to the broader subject of ONLINE PRIVACY,
of which it is one layer among many. A VPN addresses exactly one
part of the problem, who can see your traffic in transit and
what address you appear to come from. It does nothing about the
accounts you log in to, the cookies and fingerprints that
identify your browser, the apps that broadcast your location,
the data brokers that merge your records, or the permissions you
grant without reading. Privacy is built in layers, and a VPN is
a single, useful, narrow layer. The error is to install one and
consider the matter closed.

Which brings the subject back to a usable summary. A VPN is an
encrypted tunnel to a server you chose, that hides your traffic
from the network you are on and swaps your address for the
server's. It is excellent on untrusted wi-fi, useful against a
nosy internet provider, handy for shifting your apparent
location, and the right tool for reaching a private network from
afar. It relocates your trust onto the VPN company, so the
choice of company is the whole decision: prefer paid providers
with recent independent audits and, ideally, a track record
proven by a seizure that turned up nothing. And hold the limits
firmly in mind. A VPN is not anonymity, not a substitute for the
lock in the browser, and not a shield against an adversary aimed
at you personally. Used for what it is, it is a sharp and
valuable tool. Mistaken for what it is not, it is a false sense
of safety, which is worse than none.
