THE BYZANTINE GENERALS PROBLEM

WHAT IT IS

In 1982 Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak, and Marshall Pease published a
paper posing a deceptively simple puzzle. Several divisions of the
Byzantine army surround an enemy city. Each division is commanded by a
general. The generals can only communicate by messenger. Some fraction
of the generals are traitors. All the loyal generals must agree on the
same plan of action, attack or retreat, because a half-hearted,
uncoordinated attack is worse than either unified option. The traitors
will say whatever it takes to prevent that agreement: telling one
general attack, another retreat, forging messages, staying silent when
silence helps them. The question: is there an algorithm that guarantees
all loyal generals settle on the same decision, regardless of what the
traitors do.

This is not a story about ancient warfare, it is a proof about
computers. Replace generals with machines in a network, messengers with
network messages, and traitors with components that fail in arbitrary,
adversarial, or buggy ways, not just crashing cleanly but sending
contradictory data to different peers, and you have described the
central problem of distributed computing. How do independent machines
agree on anything when some of them cannot be trusted to tell the
truth, or even to fail politely.

The paper matters because it named and formalized a failure mode worse
than a machine simply going offline. A crashed machine is easy to
reason about, it stops. A Byzantine machine keeps running and keeps
lying, possibly differently to each listener. Lamport picked the
general metaphor because it made an abstract fault tolerance question
vivid, and it worked well enough that Byzantine fault became the
standard term for this entire class of failure, decades before anyone
was mining a block.

WHY IT MATTERS

Every system where multiple machines must act as one depends on solving
some version of this problem. Flight control computers on an aircraft,
redundant computers on a spacecraft, database replicas agreeing on the
order of transactions, clusters electing a leader, power grid control
systems, multi party financial settlement. Wherever the cost of
disagreement is catastrophic and components cannot be blindly trusted,
this problem sits underneath the design, whether or not anyone calls it
by name.

Before Lamport's paper, engineers built redundancy the naive way. Run
three copies of a system, take the majority vote, done. That works
fine if the only failure is a copy crashing or going silent. It falls
apart the moment a faulty component can produce different, self serving
output to different observers, which is exactly what a corrupted
sensor, a compromised node, or a bug triggered by a specific input can
do. The paper's core result is a hard limit on when majority voting, or
any agreement protocol, can work at all. That limit is a number, not a
vibe, and it is the single most load bearing fact in the whole field.

The number: to tolerate f Byzantine generals you need at least 3f+1
generals total, and the messaging needs at least 2f+1 rounds of
exchange under the oral message model. One traitor among three loyal
generals is unsolvable, three total is not enough, you need four. Two
traitors need at least seven total. This is not an engineering
shortfall waiting on a cleverer algorithm. Lamport, Shostak and Pease
proved it as an impossibility result. Below that ratio no algorithm
exists, full stop. That is the part people building systems have to
internalize before they design anything: the fault tolerance ceiling is
fixed by arithmetic, not by how good your code is.

THE CORE MECHANISM

The original paper gives two related solutions, and the shape of both
recurs everywhere downstream.

Oral Message algorithm, OM(m). Assume messages can be authenticated as
coming from whoever sent them in that hop, you know who spoke to you,
but content cannot be verified as true, and a traitor can say different
things to different people. The algorithm works recursively. The
commanding general sends his order to every lieutenant. Each lieutenant
then acts as a commander for a smaller version of the same problem,
relaying what he received to every other lieutenant, who does the same
one level down, for m levels, where m is the number of potential
traitors you want to tolerate. Each lieutenant ends up holding a whole
tree of reported values, what everyone claims everyone else claims the
order was, and takes the majority value across that tree as the final
decision. The recursion lets a lieutenant cross check "he told me X"
against "did anyone else hear him say X" through enough independent
paths that a lying minority gets outvoted by the structure of
who-said-what, not by any one message being individually verified.

Signed Message algorithm, SM(m). Assume unforgeable digital signatures
instead. Now a traitor cannot put words in a loyal general's mouth, any
message claiming to be from someone can be checked. This drops the
requirement from 3f+1 generals to just f+2, any number of traitors can
be tolerated among any group size as long as there are at least two
loyal generals, because forgery, not disagreement, was doing most of
the damage in the unsigned case. This is the direct ancestor of every
consensus system that relies on cryptographic signatures rather than
raw voting to establish who really said what.

The general shape underneath both variants: gather claims from
multiple independent paths, apply a deterministic rule, majority, or
enough matching signed claims, to collapse conflicting reports into one
decision, and structure the rounds so a bounded number of liars cannot
force two honest nodes into different conclusions. Every practical
consensus protocol since is a variation on that shape, tuned for
different assumptions about the network, synchronous or not, the
failure model, crash only versus Byzantine, and the cost you are
willing to pay in messages and rounds.

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, PBFT, published by Miguel Castro
and Barbara Liskov in 1999, is the version that made this usable in
real software rather than as a theoretical result. It runs in three
phases, pre-prepare, prepare, commit, where a designated leader
proposes an order for operations and the other replicas exchange
signed votes to confirm enough of them saw the same proposal before
anyone commits to it. It tolerates up to f faulty replicas out of 3f+1
total, matching Lamport's original bound, but does it efficiently
enough for real systems, it was built for a fault tolerant network file
system, rather than requiring the exponential message blowup of naive
recursive approaches. PBFT is the bridge between the 1982 theory and
every later system, blockchain included, that claims Byzantine fault
tolerance.

WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

First: Byzantine fault tolerant does not mean handles servers crashing.
Crash fault tolerant systems, think most database replication, or
protocols like Raft and Paxos, assume a failed node stops and stays
stopped, it does not send out corrupted or contradictory data while
pretending to be fine. That assumption is reasonable for hardware
failures and buggy but not malicious software, and it lets you get away
with a much cheaper quorum, roughly 2f+1 nodes tolerate f crash
failures, not 3f+1. People often use fault tolerant as a blanket term
and blur these two categories, but they solve different problems at
different costs. If your threat model is disks dying and processes
segfaulting, you do not need Byzantine tolerance and paying for it is
wasted overhead. If your threat model includes compromised nodes,
malicious insiders, or hardware producing corrupted but plausible
output, crash tolerance is not enough and will not save you.

Second: people assume this problem was invented for or by blockchain.
It was not, it is forty-two years older than Bitcoin. Satoshi
Nakamoto's 2008 innovation was not discovering the Byzantine Generals
Problem, it was finding a new way to make the "who gets to propose
next" step expensive to fake, using proof of work, so that an open,
permissionless network with unknown and unlimited participants could
still reach agreement without the fixed, known participant list that
PBFT and its predecessors assumed. Blockchain consensus is a specific
answer to Byzantine agreement under a specific and unusual constraint,
anyone can join, no membership list, no trusted setup, not the origin
of the problem or the only way to solve it. Most of the internet's
actual infrastructure, databases, cloud control planes, aviation
systems, solves Byzantine or crash fault agreement with pre-blockchain
protocols, because they have a known, fixed set of participants and do
not need the open and permissionless version of the puzzle at all.

Third: the 3f+1 bound is often forgotten when people design redundant
systems. Three copies of anything, with majority vote, sounds robust.
It tolerates exactly zero Byzantine faults, one bad actor among three
deadlocks or corrupts the vote, because the other two loyal generals
cannot outvote a liar two to one when the liar tells each of them
something different and there is no way to independently check who is
lying. This is the single most common real world design mistake
traceable straight back to not reading past the metaphor.

WHERE IT CONNECTS

Paxos, Lamport 1989, and Raft, 2014, solve the crash fault version of
distributed consensus, getting a set of trusted, non malicious replicas
to agree on an ordered log of operations. They are what most
production databases and coordination services actually run, because
most operational failures are crashes and network partitions, not
malice. Understanding these as the cheaper, non Byzantine cousin of the
generals problem clarifies why they need fewer replicas and less
overhead.

PBFT and its descendants, Tendermint, HotStuff, and the consensus
engines under most permissioned blockchain and enterprise distributed
ledger products, are the direct engineering lineage from the 1982
paper, built for environments where a participant might actively lie,
not just crash.

Proof of work and proof of stake blockchains solve a variant of the
same problem under an additional, harder constraint, no fixed or known
participant list, and anyone can show up pretending to be a hundred
participants, the sybil attack. Proof of work's real function is making
that pretense expensive, a different problem layered on top of
Byzantine agreement, not a replacement for it.

Aerospace and safety critical control systems, aircraft fly by wire
computers, spacecraft avionics, were doing Byzantine fault aware
redundant design around the same era as Lamport's paper, often
independently, because a sensor or flight computer that fails by lying
is a known real hazard at thirty thousand feet, and "just average three
readings" fails exactly the way the generals problem predicts if one
sensor is corrupted rather than dead.

The broader idea worth carrying out of this: trust is not free, and the
cost of trust scales in a specific, provable way with how badly a
component is allowed to misbehave. Deciding whether you are facing
crashes or lies is the first design decision in any distributed system,
and it fixes your redundancy budget before a single line of code gets
written.
