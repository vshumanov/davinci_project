SECTION 1: WHAT IT IS

Counterintelligence, CI, is the work of finding out what hostile parties know about you, what they are trying to learn, and stopping them. It is defense of information and operations against collection by adversaries.

There are two broad halves.

DEFENSIVE CI protects. It secures people, facilities, systems, and information. It trains staff, controls access, and reduces exposure.

OFFENSIVE CI acts against the adversary. It finds their agents, turns them, feeds them false information, and disrupts their operations. The Double Cross system in World War II is the model.

Insider risk is the sub-problem CI cares most about: harm done by people who already have legitimate access. An insider might steal, leak, sabotage, or simply blunder. The outside attacker must break in. The insider is already in.

Think of a security perimeter as a wall around a city. Most effort goes into the wall. The most damaging breaches in history came from someone already inside the gate with a key. That is why CI has always focused on people.

Key terms:

1) ADVERSARY: a party trying to collect your information or harm your operations. A foreign service, a competitor, a criminal group, an activist.

2) ASSET: what you are protecting. Data, plans, customer lists, code, people, reputation.

3) THREAT: capability plus intent.

4) VULNERABILITY: a weakness the threat can exploit.

5) RISK: the combination of threat, vulnerability, and impact.

6) INSIDER: anyone with authorized access, including employees, contractors, vendors, former staff.

Simple rule: you cannot protect everything equally. CI starts by deciding what matters most, and who wants it.

SECTION 2: A SHORT HISTORY

ANCIENT AND EARLY MODERN

Every state that ran spies had to worry about enemy spies. Walsingham's Elizabethan service caught plots by penetrating them. Venetian authorities monitored foreign ambassadors. The pattern is old: a good intelligence service is also a good counterintelligence service, since to catch spies you think like one.

THE FIRST WORLD WAR AND THE BIRTH OF MODERN CI

Britain's MI5, founded in 1909 as a response to fears of German espionage, rounded up the German spy network in Britain at the start of World War I, an early success. Public spy hysteria followed in several countries, a warning about overreach that repeats in every era.

THE SOVIET TRUST AND THE SOVIET LEGACY

After the Russian Revolution, the Soviet Cheka, later the OGPU, NKVD, and KGB, ran an operation called the Trust in the 1920s. It was a fake anti-Soviet resistance organization created to lure and expose émigré opponents. Counterintelligence as bait and control: a hallmark of the Soviet approach, and it worked for years.

The Soviets also excelled at penetrating Western services with long-term agents. The Cambridge Five, recruited in the 1930s on ideological grounds, reached senior positions in British intelligence and diplomacy: Kim Philby, Guy Burgess, Donald Maclean, Anthony Blunt, and John Cairncross. They passed vast material for years. Philby rose to run British counterintelligence against the Soviets while working for them. The case is the textbook lesson in the mole problem and the cost of trust based on class and background rather than verification.

THE DOUBLE CROSS SYSTEM

British counterintelligence in World War II achieved something unusual. It captured essentially every German agent sent to Britain and either turned them or controlled them. The Twenty Committee, whose Roman numeral XX gave the operation its name, ran double agents feeding the Germans a mix of true and false information. Before D-Day, the deception about the invasion location, Operation Fortitude, relied on this network. Its success rested on tight control, coordination between agents so their stories agreed, and a steady drip of believable material.

The lesson: once you control the adversary's channel, you can shape their picture of the world.

THE COLD WAR MOLE HUNTS

The Cold War produced paranoia as a byproduct. James Jesus Angleton, head of CIA counterintelligence from the 1950s to 1974, became obsessed with the idea of a Soviet mole at the top of the agency. His hunts wrecked careers and paralyzed Soviet operations for years, without finding the mole he sought. The episode is a standard case of CI turning on itself. Vigilance without evidentiary discipline damages the institution.

Meanwhile real penetrations went undetected. Aldrich Ames, a CIA officer, spied for the Soviets and then Russia from 1985 to 1994, and exposed many US sources, several of whom were executed. He lived well beyond his salary, and it took years for that to trigger serious attention. Robert Hanssen, an FBI agent, spied for Moscow for about 22 years, and was himself in a position to know how the FBI hunted for moles. Both were caught, in part, through outside information from a defector or through mistakes. Both cases led to reforms: better financial disclosure checks, polygraph and reinvestigation programs, and more attention to behavioral indicators.

THE EAST BLOC AND MASS SURVEILLANCE

The Stasi treated counterintelligence as a total system. It monitored the population for hostile intent, with informants in workplaces, apartment blocks, and families. It is the negative example of CI without limits.

ECONOMIC ESPIONAGE AND THE INSIDER EMPHASIS

After the Cold War, attention shifted to economic espionage and to a new threat, leaks of large volumes of data by trusted insiders. In the 2000s and 2010s, disclosures by insiders with system access, including the Chelsea Manning and Edward Snowden cases, showed how one person with broad access to digital systems could copy enormous quantities of material. The response in government was insider threat programs, monitoring of privileged users, and tighter access controls. Debate about civil liberties, whistleblower protection, and proportionality accompanied them.

In the private sector, companies faced insiders stealing trade secrets on the way out the door. Cases involving engineers copying source code or design files before joining a competitor became common enough to shape law and policy. Prosecutions under trade secret and economic espionage statutes rose.

SECTION 3: THE THREAT MODEL: WHO COLLECTS AND WHY

Before defense, you need to know the opponent. For a small business or engineer, the threat landscape looks different from a national government, but the categories are the same.

1) FOREIGN INTELLIGENCE SERVICES. Interested in defense, dual-use technology, critical infrastructure, and access to larger targets via smaller suppliers. Small firms are often the soft way in to a large partner. A contractor supplying a bigger company can be attacked to reach that company.

2) COMPETITORS. Interested in pricing, client lists, product plans, code, and hiring your talent. Most competitive collection is lawful and public. Some crosses into theft.

3) CRIMINAL GROUPS. Interested in money, credentials, and data to sell. Ransomware crews, business email compromise fraudsters. The largest volume threat by far for small firms.

4) INSIDERS. Employees, contractors, and vendors with access. Motives vary.

5) ACTIVISTS AND HOBBYISTS. Interested in exposure or disruption, depending on your sector.

6) OPPORTUNISTS. People who stumble on access and take advantage.

For each, ask: what do they want, how would they try to get it, and what would it cost me? A small software consultancy's crown jewels might be client data, credentials to client systems, source code, and its reputation.

THE COLLECTION METHODS

Adversaries use a limited toolbox, seen across cases:

1) HUMAN: recruitment, elicitation, social engineering, planting an employee.

2) CYBER: phishing, credential theft, malware, exploiting unpatched systems.

3) PHYSICAL: theft, tailgating into buildings, dumpster diving, device tampering.

4) SUPPLY CHAIN: compromising a vendor, software dependency, or hardware to reach the real target.

5) OPEN SOURCE: collecting what you publish yourself.

6) COMMERCIAL: buying data from brokers.

Humans are the hinge. Even sophisticated cyber attacks usually begin with a person clicking something.

SECTION 4: THE MECHANISMS OF COUNTERINTELLIGENCE

CI has a shape. Professionals describe it in cycles and layers.

THE CI CYCLE

1) IDENTIFY what is valuable and what is at risk. The crown jewels.

2) ASSESS the threat: who, how, how capable.

3) ASSESS the vulnerabilities: where would they get in.

4) COUNTER: apply measures to reduce risk.

5) DETECT: monitor for signs of compromise.

6) RESPOND: contain, investigate, remediate.

7) LEARN and adjust.

It looks like a security engineering loop because it is one.

DEFENSE IN DEPTH

No single control works. Layers overlap so that failure of one does not doom the system. Layers include: policy and culture, personnel screening, physical access, network access, data classification, monitoring, and response.

NEED TO KNOW AND COMPARTMENTATION

Give people access to what their job needs, and nothing more. Government CI calls this need to know. Compartmentation splits information into separate segments so a breach in one does not open the rest. A spy in one cell cannot expose another.

Engineers know this as the principle of least privilege and network segmentation. It is the same idea with different vocabulary.

PERSONNEL SECURITY

Hiring and continuing evaluation. Government does background investigations, financial checks, and reinvestigations. In business, the lawful equivalent includes reference checks, verifying credentials, and where permitted by law, criminal background checks in relevant roles. Vetting is contextual: a person handling client money warrants more scrutiny than one stocking shelves.

SECURITY AWARENESS AND CULTURE

Most CI programs rest on training staff to recognize approaches. That means elicitation, phishing, odd requests, unusual interest in restricted areas. Reporting culture matters more than any tool: a workforce that reports suspicious contacts without fear of blame catches things early.

DECEPTION AND CANARIES

Offensive CI tactics have defensive analogues. A canary is a trap: a unique piece of fake data planted to see who accesses or leaks it. A honeypot is a system designed to attract attackers and reveal them. Canary tokens, unique fake credentials, and watermarked documents all belong here. They exploit the principle that if a decoy is touched, nobody legitimate should have done it.

A simple documentary variant: give a slightly different version of a sensitive document to each recipient. If it leaks, the variant identifies the source. This is old spycraft known as a barium meal or canary trap, and it is legal to use on your own documents, with fair notice in your policies.

INVESTIGATION

When a compromise is suspected, CI investigates. Steps include preserving evidence, building a timeline, identifying who had access, and testing hypotheses. Investigators work to avoid tipping off the suspect prematurely. Good practice includes involving legal counsel and HR early.

THE MOLE HUNT PROBLEM

Angleton's story shows the danger. When the search for a hidden enemy becomes the organization's dominant activity, it eats trust and productivity, and produces false accusations. Good CI keeps proportionality and evidence standards. It uses indicators, not hunches, and it protects the innocent from being destroyed by suspicion.

SECTION 5: INSIDER RISK: THE PROBLEM INSIDE THE WALLS

Insider risk is an old problem, and researchers have studied it systematically. The main findings are humbling and practical.

TYPES OF INSIDERS

1) THE MALICIOUS INSIDER: deliberately steals, leaks, or sabotages. Motives include money, revenge, ideology, ego, and coercion. Recall MICE: Money, Ideology, Compromise, Ego.

2) THE NEGLIGENT INSIDER: causes harm through carelessness. Loses a laptop, emails the wrong file, clicks a phishing link. Statistically the largest category.

3) THE COMPROMISED INSIDER: a legitimate user whose credentials or device an outside attacker controls. From the system's perspective it looks like an insider.

4) THE DEPARTING INSIDER: someone leaving who takes data, clients, or code with them. Very common in commercial settings, often not fully malicious in the person's own mind: I wrote it, so it is mine.

WHAT THE RESEARCH SAYS

Studies of insider cases, such as those from the CERT Insider Threat Center at Carnegie Mellon, find recurring patterns:

1) INSIDER HARM IS RARELY SUDDEN. It typically follows a trigger, such as a demotion, a dispute, a denied promotion, a financial crisis, or an impending departure.

2) BEHAVIORAL WARNING SIGNS OFTEN EXIST. Colleagues frequently noticed something but did not report it, or reported it and nothing happened.

3) TECHNICAL SIGNS ARE COMMON IN HINDSIGHT. Unusual data access, off-hours activity, large downloads, use of removable media, and access to systems beyond their role.

4) MOST THEFT OF INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY HAPPENS WITHIN A WINDOW AROUND RESIGNATION, often the last month or so of employment.

5) SABOTAGE CASES OFTEN FOLLOW A GRIEVANCE, and technical staff with privileged access are overrepresented.

6) MANY INCIDENTS INVOLVE PEOPLE WITH ACCESS THE JOB NEVER REQUIRED. Over-permissioned accounts are the enabling condition.

None of this predicts who will do it. Base rates are low. Treating every stressed employee as a suspect is both unfair and ineffective. The right approach is to lower the opportunity and to detect anomalies in behavior, not to judge character.

THE FRAUD TRIANGLE

Donald Cressey's fraud triangle explains why trusted people cross lines. Three elements: pressure, opportunity, and rationalization. Remove one and risk falls. Businesses control opportunity best, with separation of duties and audit. Pressure can be reduced by fair treatment and support. Rationalization is reduced by clear norms and consequences.

INDICATORS

Frameworks list behavioral and technical indicators. A cautious summary:

BEHAVIORAL: expressed grievance, conflict with management, sudden unexplained wealth, interest in matters outside their duties, resisting oversight, working odd hours without reason, sudden financial stress, statements about leaving with something.

TECHNICAL: bulk downloads, access to unfamiliar repositories, use of personal cloud or email for work files, copying to USB, disabling security tools, use of unauthorized software, attempts to access restricted data, use of another person's credentials.

Any single indicator is weak. Clusters and changes from baseline matter. A programmer working late before a deadline is normal. A programmer who never worked late and suddenly bulk-exports repositories at 3 a.m. after a bad review is a different picture.

THE IMPORTANCE OF FAIRNESS

Insider risk programs can become surveillance regimes that corrode trust. The best programs are transparent about monitoring, proportionate, legally reviewed, and paired with support. An organization that treats staff as suspects breeds the resentment that creates insiders. Aim for a culture where people feel treated fairly and can raise concerns. Fairness is a security control.

SECTION 6: WHAT PEOPLE GET WRONG

MISTAKE 1: THE THREAT IS THE HACKER IN THE HOODIE

For most small firms the realistic threats are phishing, credential reuse, business email compromise, ransomware, a careless employee, and a departing one. Exotic spies are rare. Basics beat sophistication.

MISTAKE 2: WE ARE TOO SMALL TO BE A TARGET

Small firms are targets because they are soft. Attackers use automation and target by opportunity. Small suppliers are also stepping stones to bigger clients.

MISTAKE 3: TRUST IS A CONTROL

Trust is a relationship, not a control. The Cambridge Five were trusted because of who they were. Verification is not an insult. It is what lets trust be safe. Two-person approval for payments is not an accusation.

MISTAKE 4: SCREENING ONCE IS ENOUGH

People change. Circumstances change. Ames's finances shifted visibly for years. Access should be reviewed periodically, and privileges removed when roles change.

MISTAKE 5: TECHNOLOGY SOLVES INSIDER RISK

Monitoring tools produce alerts. Humans must interpret them and act fairly. Tools without process create noise and liability.

MISTAKE 6: ONLY MALICE MATTERS

Negligence is the bigger volume. Design systems that make the safe path the easy path.

MISTAKE 7: MORE SUSPICION MEANS MORE SECURITY

The Angleton lesson. Suspicion without evidence discipline destroys good staff and does not find spies. Proportion and process protect the innocent and improve accuracy.

MISTAKE 8: A LEAK MEANS A SPY

Many leaks are careless, or come from someone who was talkative, or from a system misconfiguration. Look at simple explanations before spy stories.

MISTAKE 9: THE EXIT IS A HANDSHAKE

Departure is the highest-risk moment. Cutting access on the last day, not later, and doing a structured offboarding is basic, and often skipped.

SECTION 7: LEGAL EVERYDAY USE FOR A SMALL BUSINESS OWNER AND ENGINEER

You are not running a counterespionage service. You are running a company with client data, code, and money. Apply CI thinking at small scale.

STEP 1: KNOW YOUR CROWN JEWELS

Write a list of the five to ten things that would hurt most if lost, leaked, or altered. Client data, source code, credentials, financial accounts, contracts, key staff knowledge. Rank them. Note where each lives and who can access it. This is the asset inventory. Most firms have never written it.

STEP 2: KNOW WHO WANTS THEM

For each, ask who might want it and how. Criminals want money and credentials. Competitors want client lists and pricing. Departing staff want code and contacts. A short table, in your head or on paper, is enough.

STEP 3: LEAST PRIVILEGE AND SEGMENTATION

1) Each person gets access only to what their role needs.

2) Separate admin accounts from daily accounts.

3) Keep production credentials out of shared chat and personal notes. Use a password manager with shared vaults.

4) Segment networks, so a guest or an IoT device cannot reach business systems.

5) Review access every quarter and remove what is unused.

This one measure blunts a large share of insider and external threats.

STEP 4: TWO-PERSON RULES FOR HIGH-IMPACT ACTIONS

Payments above a threshold, changes to bank details, production database changes, and releasing client credentials should need a second person or a second channel. It defeats both fraud and the compromised account. It is a normal control in finance and safety engineering.

STEP 5: PHISHING AND BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE DEFENSE

The top real-world threat. Rules:

1) Multi-factor authentication everywhere, preferably hardware keys or authenticator apps rather than SMS.

2) Any change of payment details is verified by phone on a known number.

3) Urgent requests from the boss for money or gift cards are verified.

4) Staff know how to report suspicious mail with no blame.

STEP 6: HIRING AND ONBOARDING

Use lawful background checks appropriate to the role and jurisdiction. Verify references and claimed qualifications. Have new hires sign confidentiality and IP assignment agreements. Explain the rules on day one. Check local law on what checks are permitted.

STEP 7: CLEAR AGREEMENTS

Written contracts define confidentiality, ownership of work, and return of materials. Non-disclosure agreements with clients and vendors. In some jurisdictions non-competes are restricted, so take legal advice. Clear rules reduce the rationalization leg of the fraud triangle: people cannot claim they did not know.

STEP 8: TRANSPARENT MONITORING

Log access to critical systems. Alert on bulk exports, new admin accounts, and logins from odd locations. Tell staff that logging exists, in policy. Where staff personal data is involved, follow data protection law: proportionality, purpose limitation, and notice. In the EU, workplace monitoring has specific limits. Ask a lawyer before deploying anything invasive, such as screen recording or keystroke logging. Log the systems, not the people's private lives.

STEP 9: CANARIES AND TRIPWIRES

Cheap and legal on your own systems. Plant a fake client record, a fake credential, or a canary token file in sensitive locations. If touched, you get an alert. Unique document versions to different recipients can trace a leak. Note these in policy so nobody is surprised.

STEP 10: OFFBOARDING

A checklist for every departure, voluntary or not:

1) Revoke accounts and tokens the same day.

2) Collect devices and keys.

3) Rotate shared credentials the person knew.

4) Review recent access and downloads for the final weeks.

5) Remind them in writing of confidentiality duties.

6) Transfer knowledge and ownership of projects.

7) Treat them well. Bitter departures create the risk you are guarding against.

STEP 11: CONTRACTORS AND VENDORS

Contractors are insiders with fewer loyalties. Give them scoped, time-limited access. Use separate accounts. Log their activity. Check the security of vendors that touch client data, and ask for basic assurance, such as security questionnaires or recognized certifications. Remember supply chain attacks: your software dependencies and tools are also vendors.

STEP 12: A REPORTING CHANNEL AND A CULTURE

Make it easy and safe to report a mistake or a concern. A person who clicks a bad link and reports it in five minutes saves you. A person who hides it because of fear costs you a breach. Praise reporting. Blameless postmortems for incidents.

STEP 13: FAIR TREATMENT

Pay fairly. Communicate honestly about changes. Address grievances early. Most insider harm has a grievance in the background. Being decent is a security control with a good return.

STEP 14: WHEN SOMETHING HAPPENS

1) Preserve evidence. Do not wipe machines.

2) Limit who knows.

3) Involve a lawyer early.

4) Consider reporting to police or to the relevant data protection authority. In the EU, personal data breaches often carry a notification deadline of 72 hours.

5) Do not confront a suspected insider alone or accuse without evidence.

6) Do not retaliate or break the law yourself. Hacking back is illegal.

STEP 15: ENGINEERS: PROTECT YOUR OWN WORK

If you are a contractor or employee, know what you own. Keep client and personal work separate. Do not copy client material to personal storage. Return everything at the end. Clean separation protects you legally and professionally.

BOUNDARIES

CI thinking must not become a private intelligence operation against people. Avoid covertly surveilling staff, hiring investigators to dig into their private lives, or entrapping them. Follow employment, privacy, and criminal law. If you suspect serious wrongdoing, use lawful channels: HR, counsel, and police.

SECTION 8: WHERE IT CONNECTS

ELICITATION AND HUMAN INTELLIGENCE

The offense whose defense is CI. Awareness of elicitation is the most effective single human control.

ANALYTIC TRADECRAFT

CI investigations rely on hypothesis testing, timelines, link analysis, and disciplined source evaluation. ACH was made for questions like: who leaked this?

SURVEILLANCE DETECTION AND OPSEC

Detection spots collection in progress. Opsec reduces exposure. CI is the umbrella program that runs all of it.

INFORMATION SECURITY

Cybersecurity frameworks like zero trust, least privilege, and identity governance are CI principles in engineering form. Zero trust says do not assume the inside is safe, which is the Philby lesson.

FRAUD EXAMINATION

Forensic accounting and fraud research offer tools for detecting internal theft. The fraud triangle comes from there.

LAW

Trade secret law, employment law, data protection, and criminal statutes on unauthorized access define what you can do and what protections you have.

ORGANIZATIONAL PSYCHOLOGY

Trust, justice, and morale shape insider behavior. The Angleton case is also a study in institutional pathology.

READING LIST FOR LATER

1) A Spy Among Friends, by Ben Macintyre, on Kim Philby and the cost of misplaced trust.

2) Double Cross, by Ben Macintyre, on the WWII deception system.

3) Public guidance from national cyber and counterintelligence agencies on insider threat, freely available.

4) The CERT Guide to Insider Threats, for the research base.

5) Spy the Lie or similar interviewing texts, with a skeptical eye, for context on detecting deception.

THE SHORT VERSION

CI protects what matters from those who want it, and the most dangerous adversary is often already inside. The history teaches two opposite lessons: misplaced trust destroys, and paranoia destroys. Hold both.

Inventory your crown jewels. Give minimum access. Require a second person for high-impact actions. Log and tell people you log. Offboard fast. Treat people fairly. Keep suspicion evidence-based.
